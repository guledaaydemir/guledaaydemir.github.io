Breathing techniques to fall asleep faster

By Gül Eda Aydemir, indie iOS developer · Updated 15 Oct 2026 · 6 min read

Short answer

Use any pattern where the exhale is longer than the inhale, and keep it slow — around six breaths a minute or fewer. Four seconds in and eight seconds out is the simplest version. 4-7-8 adds a hold and works well if holding is comfortable for you. Practise for five minutes without checking whether it is working.

What do sleep breathing techniques have in common?

Most sleep patterns slow the rate, and many lengthen the exhale: 4-7-8 and the physiological sigh both end on a long out-breath, while coherent breathing keeps the phases equal and simply slows you down. So the working rule is: slow the rate down, and if it feels comfortable, make the exhale longer than the inhale. Much of the rest is packaging.

Heart rate rises slightly during the inhale and falls during the exhale. Spend more of each breath exhaling and you spend more of each minute on the settling side of that cycle. Slowing the overall rate adds to the effect. That is the core idea, and it is why you do not need to learn several techniques.

The research backs slow breathing in general rather than any one count: a 2018 systematic review linked slow breathing with more “rest and digest” (parasympathetic) activity and lower anxiety and arousal (Zaccaro et al., see Sources).

Which breathing pattern should I use for sleep?

Start with four in, eight out. Switch to 4-7-8 if holds feel easy, to 4 in, 6 out if long exhales feel tight, or to coherent breathing (5 in, 5 out) if you want something even and simple.

  • Four-eight is the default. No hold, nothing to get wrong, sustainable for ten minutes.
  • 4-7-8 is worth trying if the seven-second hold feels comfortable. It is not “stronger” than the others: the research supports slow breathing in general, not one count. If the hold makes you tense, it is the wrong pattern for you.
  • Extended exhale (4 in, 6 out) is the gentlest entry point, and the one to use if longer exhales make you feel short of breath.
  • Coherent breathing uses equal phases at a slow rate. Good for general settling, slightly less sleep-specific than the others.

How do I run it in bed?

Lie down in the position you sleep in, pick one pattern and keep it going for about five minutes without checking whether it is working. The step-by-step version (exhale first, fill to about seventy percent, let the out-breath fall out) is in I can’t sleep at night: what to do tonight.

  • One pattern per night. Switching mid-session turns breathing back into a decision.
  • Give it a few nights. With repetition, the same pattern can become a familiar cue for sleep.
  • No target count. Counting toward a number of breaths is goal-checking, and goal-checking keeps you awake.

Which breathing techniques should I avoid before bed?

Skip fast, forceful breathing and long holds on empty lungs: they are energising rather than settling.

  • Fast or forceful breathing. Wim Hof style breathing, breath of fire, hyperventilation-based methods — these raise alertness by design. They belong in the morning, not at midnight.
  • Long breath holds after the exhale. Empty-lung holds create air hunger, which is the opposite of what you want at bedtime.
  • Straining for the count. If eight seconds requires effort, use six. A strained long exhale raises tension and cancels the benefit.
  • Checking progress. The moment you ask whether it is working, you have reintroduced vigilance. Let it be boring.

How long does it take to work?

Within a single session, many people feel a little calmer within a few minutes. Falling asleep is a separate event and may still take another ten or twenty minutes — the breathing removes an obstacle, it does not trigger sleep on command.

Across weeks, there is a second benefit: with repetition, the pattern can become a cue for sleep. Breathing the same way in bed night after night gives you a familiar signal that the day is over. That is worth more than any single night's result, and it is the argument for attaching it to a short, fixed routine.

Common questions

Which breathing technique is best for sleep?

Any slow pattern with the exhale longer than the inhale. 4-7-8 is the best known, but four in and eight out is easier to sustain, and the research supports slow breathing in general rather than one specific count.

How many breaths does it take to fall asleep?

There is no fixed number. Practise for around five minutes rather than counting to a target — counting to a target reintroduces the goal-checking that keeps you awake.

Should I breathe through my nose or mouth?

Inhale through the nose. Exhale through either, though a slightly pursed mouth makes slowing down easier. Once you stop practising, nasal breathing is preferable.
