How to stop racing thoughts at bedtime

By Gül Eda Aydemir, indie iOS developer · Updated 15 Oct 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

Give your mind one slow, countable job: breathe in for 4 and out for 6 to 8, for two to five minutes, lights off and eyes closed. The longer out-breath is the part that settles you; the counting is the part that crowds out the thoughts. If the same worry keeps coming back, write one specific next step on paper, then return to the breath. If you’d rather not count, ATHA’s Eyes-closed mode dims the screen and gentle haptics mark each breath.

Why do racing thoughts start the moment I lie down?

Because the distractions stop, not because the thoughts start. Nothing new arrives at bedtime. All day, meetings, messages, traffic and noise consume your attention continuously. Lying down in a dark, silent room removes every external demand simultaneously — and whatever was queued up behind them finally gets processed.

That is why the thoughts feel like they arrived. They didn't. The distraction left.

There is a second reason the thoughts feel more serious at 3am: you are tired, alone and in the dark, which is a poor setting for judging anything. A problem that looks catastrophic at 3am and manageable at 9am has not changed. You have.

What actually stops racing thoughts at night?

Three things, in this order: give your attention a dull job (a slow, counted breath), close the open loops on paper, and change rooms if you’ve been stuck for about twenty minutes.

1. Give attention a dull job

Suppression fails because monitoring for absence of thought is itself effortful attention. Occupation tends to work better, because it is hard to follow a count and a worry at the same time. Counting a slow breath is close to perfect for this: repetitive, slightly effortful, and with no result to evaluate.

  1. Count inside the breath, not the breathsOne-two-three-four in, one-through-eight out. Counting cycles creates a target; counting seconds does not.
  2. Expect to lose the countYou will drift. That is not failure — noticing and returning is the practice. Restart from wherever you are, without commentary.
  3. Keep the exhale longFour in, eight out. This is what lowers the arousal underneath the thinking, not just the thinking itself.
  4. Give it five minutesThe first two minutes often feel like nothing is happening. Keep going anyway; this is not a test you can fail.

2. Close the open loops, specifically

Many bedtime thoughts are not worries at all. They are unfinished tasks your memory is keeping active in case you forget them. These respond well to being written down — but only if you write the right thing.

"Deal with the invoice" stays open. "Email Anna the invoice at 9am" is closed.

Vague notes don't release the loop, because your mind still has to work out what the action is. Writing specific next steps often helps more than trying not to think about them: in a small sleep-lab study, people who spent five minutes writing a specific to-do list fell asleep faster than people who wrote about tasks they had already finished (Scullin et al.).

3. Change the room, not the position

If twenty minutes have passed and you are cycling the same three thoughts with rising frustration, stop. Get up, keep the lights low, do something undemanding in another room, and return when you feel sleepy. Lying in bed rehearsing worry teaches your brain that bed is where you rehearse worry. The Sleep Foundation gives the same advice for night waking: after about 20 minutes, get up and do something quiet until you feel sleepy. Woken in the small hours? Here’s the 3am version.

What makes racing thoughts worse?

Anything that gives your brain new material or a deadline: your phone, the clock, and trying to solve things at 3am.

  • Your phone. Not only the light: mostly the content. Checking one message reopens every loop you just closed.
  • Solving the problem at 3am. You have neither the information nor the judgement. Note the next step, not the solution.
  • Clock-watching. Calculating how much sleep remains converts a quiet night into a countdown. Turn the clock away.
  • Getting annoyed at the thoughts. Frustration is arousal, and arousal is the thing keeping you awake. Boredom is the target, not silence.

When are racing thoughts worth talking to a doctor about?

Racing thoughts that persist for weeks, arrive with a pounding heart or dread, or come with several nights of needing very little sleep while feeling energised, are worth discussing with a doctor rather than managing alone. The same applies if the thoughts are mainly low, hopeless or self-critical. These are treatable, and breathing practice is a companion to that, not a replacement. If you have thoughts of harming yourself, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line now (for example Samaritans on 116 123 in the UK, or call or text 988 in the US).

Common questions

Why do I overthink at night but not during the day?

Daytime demands occupy your attention continuously. Lying in the dark removes all of them at once, leaving whatever is unresolved to surface. The thoughts were always there; the distraction is what changed.

How do I stop thinking so I can sleep?

You can’t stop on command; trying is itself a thought. Give your attention something undemanding and repetitive to hold instead, such as the seconds of a slow breath. Occupation tends to work where suppression fails.

What’s the best breathing exercise for racing thoughts?

A slow, counted one with a longer out-breath: 4 in and 6 to 8 out for two to five minutes. Counting the seconds keeps your attention busy; the long exhale settles the arousal underneath the thinking.

Does writing things down before bed help?

It can, especially for thoughts that are really unfinished tasks. In a small sleep-lab study, people who wrote a specific to-do list for five minutes before bed fell asleep faster than people who wrote about tasks they had already finished. Write a specific next step rather than a vague worry.

Should I get out of bed if I can’t stop thinking?

After about 20 minutes, yes, especially if frustration is building. Keep the lights low, do something dull in another room, and come back when you feel sleepy.
