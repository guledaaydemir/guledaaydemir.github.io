How to stop racing thoughts at bedtime

Short answer

You cannot stop thoughts by deciding to — the decision is itself a thought. What works is occupying your attention with something repetitive and undemanding. Counting the seconds of a slow breath is ideal, because it is dull, it has no outcome to worry about, and it lowers arousal at the same time. If the same worry keeps returning, write down one concrete next step for it, then go back to the breath.

Why it happens only at night

Nothing new arrives at bedtime. All day, meetings, messages, traffic and noise consume your attention continuously. Lying down in a dark, silent room removes every external demand simultaneously — and whatever was queued up behind them finally gets processed.

That is why the thoughts feel like they arrived. They didn't. The distraction left.

There is a second reason the thoughts feel more serious at 3am: judgement genuinely is worse then. Sleep deprivation and low-light isolation both tilt interpretation toward the negative. A problem that looks catastrophic at 3am and manageable at 9am has not changed. You have.

Three things that actually interrupt it

1. Give attention a dull job

Suppression fails because monitoring for absence of thought is itself effortful attention. Occupation succeeds because attention is largely single-channel. Counting a slow breath is close to perfect for this: repetitive, slightly effortful, and with no result to evaluate.

  1. Count inside the breath, not the breathsOne-two-three-four in, one-through-eight out. Counting cycles creates a target; counting seconds does not.
  2. Expect to lose the countYou will drift. That is not failure — noticing and returning is the practice. Restart from wherever you are, without commentary.
  3. Keep the exhale longFour in, eight out. This is what lowers the arousal underneath the thinking, not just the thinking itself.
  4. Give it five minutesThe first two minutes usually feel like it isn't working. It is the third and fourth that do the work.

2. Close the open loops, specifically

A large share of bedtime thoughts are not worries at all. They are unfinished tasks your memory is keeping active in case you forget them. These respond well to being written down — but only if you write the right thing.

"Deal with the invoice" stays open. "Email Anna the invoice at 9am" is closed.

Vague notes don't release the loop, because your mind still has to work out what the action is. Five specific next-steps on paper, two minutes before bed, clears more mental noise than an hour of trying not to think about them.

3. Change the room, not the position

If twenty minutes have passed and you are cycling the same three thoughts with rising frustration, stop. Get up, keep the lights low, do something undemanding in another room, and return when you feel sleepy. Lying in bed rehearsing worry teaches your brain that bed is where you rehearse worry.

What to avoid

  • Your phone. Not because of blue light — because of content. Checking one message reopens every loop you just closed.
  • Solving the problem at 3am. You have neither the information nor the judgement. Note the next step, not the solution.
  • Clock-watching. Calculating how much sleep remains converts a quiet night into a countdown. Turn the clock away.
  • Getting annoyed at the thoughts. Frustration is arousal, and arousal is the thing keeping you awake. Boredom is the target, not silence.

When it's more than bedtime overthinking

Racing thoughts that persist for weeks, arrive with a pounding heart or dread, or come with several nights of needing very little sleep while feeling energised, are worth discussing with a doctor rather than managing alone. The same applies if the thoughts are mainly low, hopeless or self-critical. These are treatable, and breathing practice is a companion to that, not a replacement.

Common questions

Why do I overthink at night but not during the day?

Daytime demands occupy your attention continuously. Lying in the dark removes all of them at once, leaving whatever is unresolved to surface. The thoughts were always there — the distraction is what changed.

How do I stop thinking so I can sleep?

You can't stop on command; trying is itself a thought. Give attention something undemanding and repetitive to hold instead — the seconds of a slow breath work well. Occupation succeeds where suppression fails.

Does writing things down before bed help?

Yes, especially for thoughts that are really unfinished tasks. Write a specific next step rather than a vague worry — that converts an open loop into something scheduled.
