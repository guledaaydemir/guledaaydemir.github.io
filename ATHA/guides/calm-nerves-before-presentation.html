How to calm nerves before a presentation: a 10-minute plan

By Gül Eda Aydemir, indie iOS developer · Updated 15 Oct 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

Use the last ten minutes on purpose. At T-10, do two minutes of box breathing (in 4, hold 4, out 4, hold 4) to steady yourself without getting drowsy. At T-3, say your first sentence once, quietly. At T-0, take one slow breath in and a long breath out, then start talking on the out-breath. If your mind goes blank mid-talk, pause and take two slow breaths: a short silence feels far longer to you than to the room.

What’s the fastest way to calm presentation nerves?

Slow, deliberate breathing, plus knowing exactly what you’ll do in the next ten minutes. The advice in Mayo Clinic’s health library is refreshingly low-tech: take two or more deep, slow breaths before you get up to the podium and during your speech, and rehearse your presentation several times beforehand, ideally in front of people you’re comfortable with.

Nerves themselves aren’t the enemy. A racing heart before a talk is your body getting ready to do something that matters. The goal isn’t to feel nothing; it’s to bring the volume down enough that your voice and your memory still work.

The T-minus 10 minutes plan

  1. T-1 day
    Rehearse out loud, twiceOnce alone, once to a friend or a camera. Then do the 2-minute breathing routine once, so it’s familiar tomorrow.
  2. T-10
    Two minutes of box breathingIn 4, hold 4, out 4, hold 4. About seven boxes. Sitting, eyes open or closed. This is your main reset.
  3. T-5
    Body checkDrop your shoulders, unclench your jaw, sip some water, put your phone on silent and out of sight.
  4. T-3
    Your first sentence, onceSay it quietly, at the speed you want to deliver it. Openings are where nerves bite hardest; know yours cold.
  5. T-1
    If your heart is still poundingSwitch to a long exhale for a minute: in 4, out 6. No holds to think about.
  6. T-0
    One breath, then goSlow breath in through the nose, long breath out, and start your first sentence at the end of that out-breath.

Box breathing or a long exhale: which one before you speak?

Box breathing for the T-10 reset, a long exhale for the last-minute wobble. Box keeps the in- and out-breath equal, which settles you while keeping you alert. Exhale-heavy breathing (4 in, 6 out) feels more settling to many people but leans towards rest, which is perfect for a minute and less perfect for twenty.

What if my voice shakes?

Give it air and time. These are small, practical fixes, not magic, but they add up:

  • Start on an out-breath, not a held one. Breathe in, let a little go, then speak.
  • Slow your first three sentences to about 80% of your normal speed. It will feel painfully slow. It won’t sound it.
  • Breathe at full stops. Mark two or three planned pauses in your notes.
  • Keep water within reach. A sip is also a free, socially acceptable pause.

What should I do if my mind goes blank?

Pause, breathe twice, look at your notes. Mayo Clinic’s health library puts it nicely: when your mind goes blank it may feel like you’ve been silent for an eternity, but in reality it’s probably only a few seconds. If you want words to fill the gap, “Let me come back to the main point” buys you time and sounds intentional. It also helps to focus on your material rather than the audience; people mainly pay attention to the new information.

Does this work for interviews, exams and auditions?

The same plan works anywhere there’s a waiting room. For an interview, do the T-10 box breathing before you go in, and use the T-0 breath before your first answer. For an exam, do it outside the room and again after you’ve read the paper. Part of the value is the routine itself: the more familiar the steps, the less there is to think about on the day.

When is it more than nerves?

If fear of speaking stops you from doing things you want or need to do, it’s worth getting help, not just better breathing. Mayo Clinic’s health library notes that cognitive behavioural therapy, a skills-based approach, can ease the fear of public speaking, and that a doctor or other healthcare professional can talk through options with you. Speaking clubs such as Toastmasters are another low-stakes way to get practice.

Common questions

How long before a presentation should I do breathing exercises?

About ten minutes before: two minutes of box breathing, then one slow breath right before you start. Practise the routine once the day before so it’s familiar.

Does breathing really help with public speaking nerves?

It helps you settle, though it won’t make nerves disappear. Mayo Clinic’s health library recommends two or more deep, slow breaths before you get up and during your speech, alongside rehearsing several times.

What should I do if my mind goes blank during a talk?

Pause, take two slow breaths and look at your notes. It may feel like an eternity, but in reality it’s probably only a few seconds.

Can people tell if I’m doing a breathing exercise?

Not if you breathe through your nose and keep it gentle. With an Apple Watch, ATHA guides each phase with a tap on your wrist, so you don’t need to look at a screen.

Should I use 4-7-8 breathing before a presentation?

Probably not. Its seven-second hold is a lot to manage when you’re keyed up, and it’s designed for winding down. Box breathing or a short long-exhale breath suits performance better.
