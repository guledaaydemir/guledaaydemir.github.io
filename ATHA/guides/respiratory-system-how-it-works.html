The respiratory system: parts, functions and how it works

By Gül Eda Aydemir, indie iOS developer · Updated 3 Nov 2026 · 5 min read

Quick answer

The respiratory system is the set of organs and muscles that moves air in and out of your body, so oxygen can get into your blood and carbon dioxide can get out. Its main parts are the nose and mouth, the throat, the voice box, the windpipe, the bronchial tubes, the lungs with their tiny air sacs (alveoli), and the diaphragm, the muscle that does most of the work. It runs without you thinking about it, but you can also take the controls, which is what every breathing exercise does.

What is the respiratory system?

It is your body’s gas-exchange system. Your cells need a steady supply of oxygen and make carbon dioxide as a waste gas, and the lungs are where one is swapped for the other. NHLBI, the US lung and heart institute, calls this swap gas exchange. Everything else in the system exists to get air to that point, clean it on the way and push the old air back out.

You’ve probably searched this for a biology class, or because something felt off with your breathing. Either is fine. If it’s the second, and it’s sudden or severe, stop reading and get medical help.

How does the respiratory system work?

One breath, in five steps:

  1. The diaphragm pulls downIt sits under your lungs like a dome. As it drops, your chest gets bigger and air flows in.
  2. Air gets conditionedYour nose or mouth warms and moistens it on the way past.
  3. Air travels down the treeLarynx, windpipe, bronchi, bronchioles, each branch narrower than the last.
  4. The swap happensIn the alveoli, oxygen moves into the tiny blood vessels around each sac and carbon dioxide moves out of them.
  5. The diaphragm risesYour chest gets smaller, and the old air leaves the way it came.

Then your blood takes over. How the circulatory and respiratory systems work together covers the second half of the trip.

What are the functions of the respiratory system?

Gas exchange is the headline, but Cleveland Clinic lists a few side jobs too:

  • Warming, moistening and filtering the air you breathe
  • Making speech possible, by pushing air through the voice box
  • Helping you smell
  • Helping keep your body’s acid-base balance steady
  • Clearing irritants, through mucus, coughing and sneezing

Which part of breathing can you control?

Almost all of the time, none of it needs you. Breathing centres in your brain adjust your rate using signals from sensors that track carbon dioxide and oxygen levels. But NHLBI points out you can also change your breathing on purpose, up to a point. Holding your breath and singing are the everyday examples.

That small override is what makes breathing exercises possible. You can’t will your heart to slow down, but you can stretch an out-breath. And a 2018 review linked slow breathing, under 10 breaths a minute, with higher heart-rate variability, while noting that the studies were small and mixed. Interesting, promising, not magic.

Common questions

What are the main organs of the respiratory system?

The nose and mouth, pharynx (throat), larynx (voice box), trachea (windpipe), bronchi and bronchioles, and the lungs with their alveoli. The diaphragm is the main muscle that drives breathing.

What is the main function of the respiratory system?

Gas exchange: getting oxygen into your blood and carbon dioxide out of it. It also conditions the air you breathe, makes speech possible and helps you smell.

Where does gas exchange happen?

In the alveoli, tiny air sacs at the end of the smallest airways. Oxygen passes into the tiny blood vessels around them, and carbon dioxide passes out to be breathed away.

Why is the right lung different from the left?

The right lung has three lobes and the left has two. The left lung also has a dip that makes room for the heart.

Is breathing voluntary or involuntary?

Both. It runs automatically under the control of your brain, but you can change it on purpose for a while, such as when you hold your breath, sing or do a breathing exercise.
