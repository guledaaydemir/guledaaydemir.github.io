I can't sleep at night — what should I do?

Short answer

Stop trying to fall asleep and change what your body is doing instead. Lie still, breathe in for four seconds and out for eight, and keep going for five minutes. The long exhale lowers the alertness that is blocking sleep, and counting gives your mind a task that isn't a problem to solve. If you are still awake after about twenty minutes, get up, keep the lights low, and come back when you feel sleepy.

Why trying harder makes it worse

Sleep is not something you do. It is something that happens when the conditions are right and you stop interfering. The moment you start monitoring whether it is working — am I drifting off yet? — you have introduced effort and vigilance, which are the two states furthest from sleep.

This is why the advice to "just relax" is useless. You cannot instruct yourself into relaxation. What you can do is change something physical, and let the mental state follow. Breathing is the most direct lever available, because it is the one part of your autonomic nervous system you can operate on purpose.

Do this first: the four-eight breath

You do not need to sit up, change position, or do anything visible. Stay exactly where you are.

  1. Exhale firstEmpty your lungs through your mouth before you begin. Starting on an out-breath stops you from gulping air on the first inhale.
  2. Inhale for fourThrough the nose, slowly, letting your belly rise rather than your chest. Do not fill to capacity — around seventy percent is enough.
  3. Exhale for eightSlow and unforced, through the mouth or nose. The exhale should feel like a sigh being let out over time, not a blow.
  4. Repeat for five minutesRoughly twenty-five breaths. Do not count cycles, count the seconds within each breath. Losing count is fine — start again from wherever you are.

If eight seconds feels like straining, use four in and six out. The ratio matters far more than the absolute numbers. An exhale longer than the inhale is the whole mechanism.

Why the exhale is the part that matters

Your heart rate rises slightly on every inhale and falls on every exhale. Lengthening the out-breath relative to the in-breath tips the balance toward the recovery side of your nervous system. This is a general property of breathing, not a trick — it is why sighing feels like relief, and why people breathe fast and shallow when frightened.

Practically, this means the common instruction to "take deep breaths" is half wrong. Deep, fast breaths raise alertness. Slow breaths with a long, unhurried exhale do the opposite.

If you're still awake after twenty minutes

Do not keep lying there accumulating frustration. Every night spent awake and annoyed in bed teaches your brain that bed is a place where you lie awake and feel annoyed. That association is the thing that turns one bad night into a pattern.

  1. Get upGo to a different room if you can. Keep the lights as low as you can manage — enough to move safely, no more.
  2. Do something undemandingA dull book. Folding laundry. Sitting. Not your phone, not email, nothing with a scroll or a notification.
  3. Wait for the signalReturn to bed when you feel genuinely sleepy — heavy eyes, losing the thread of what you're reading — not when you decide enough time has passed.
  4. Breathe again once you're backFive minutes of long exhales. If it doesn't work a second time, repeat the whole loop without treating it as a failure.

What to change tomorrow, not tonight

Tonight is about getting through. These are for the daytime, when you actually have the capacity to change something:

  • Fix the wake time, not the bedtime. Getting up at the same hour every day, including weekends, does more for sleep than any bedtime rule.
  • Get daylight in the first hour after waking. Ten minutes outside sets the clock that decides when you get sleepy sixteen hours later.
  • Move the caffeine cut-off earlier. Caffeine has a half-life of around five to six hours. A 4pm coffee is still measurably present at bedtime.
  • Give the day an ending. Not a two-hour ritual — ten minutes is enough. The point is a boundary, not a spa.

When to stop self-managing this

Difficulty sleeping three or more nights a week, for three months or longer, is the standard threshold for chronic insomnia and it responds well to treatment — particularly CBT-I, which is the recommended first-line approach and outperforms sleeping medication over time. Also worth raising with a doctor: loud snoring with gasping or pauses, persistent daytime sleepiness despite adequate time in bed, or sleeplessness that arrived alongside low mood or anxiety. None of these are things to breathe your way through alone.

Common questions

How long should it take to fall asleep?

Roughly ten to twenty minutes is typical. Falling asleep the instant your head hits the pillow usually means accumulated sleep debt rather than excellent sleep. Regularly lying awake forty minutes or more is worth raising with a doctor.

Should I get out of bed if I can't sleep?

After about twenty minutes, yes — especially if frustration is building. Keep the lights low, do something undemanding, and return when you feel sleepy. This protects the association between your bed and sleeping.

Does breathing actually help you fall asleep?

It does not force sleep. What it does is lower the arousal blocking sleep, and occupy an overactive mind with something that has no outcome to worry about. That combination is usually what was missing.
