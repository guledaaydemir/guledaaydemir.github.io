How to calm down fast: a 60-second plan

By Gül Eda Aydemir, indie iOS developer · Updated 15 Oct 2026 · 7 min read

Quick answer

Make your out-breath longer than your in-breath. Breathe in through your nose for 4 seconds and out slowly for 6, shoulders down, for one to two minutes. That’s six breaths a minute. Then name five things you can see. The long exhale settles your body; the naming pulls your attention back into the room. If you’d rather not count, the timer below (or ATHA’s I need a moment button) keeps the rhythm for you.

First, a safety check

If you have chest pain or pressure, pain spreading to your arm, neck or jaw, or you feel short of breath, sweaty or faint, call your local emergency number now (999 in the UK, 911 in the US, 112 in the EU and Türkiye). Breathing exercises are for everyday stress and anxiety. They are not a treatment and not a substitute for medical help.

What’s the fastest way to calm down?

Slow your breathing down and make the exhale the long part. Breathing is the one piece of the stress response you can steer on purpose, and you can do it in a meeting, a car park or a bathroom stall without anyone noticing.

Here’s the short version of why it works. Your heart rate rises a little on every in-breath and falls a little on every out-breath. Stretch the out-breath and you spend more of each minute on the “settling” side of that swing. Slow breathing, under ten breaths a minute, is linked in the research with calmer body signals such as higher heart-rate variability, though the studies are smaller than anyone would like (a 2018 systematic review says exactly that).

What doesn’t help: big, fast “deep breaths”. Gulping air speeds you up. Slow is the whole trick.

The 60-second plan, step by step

  1. Drop your shoulders, unclench your jawTwo seconds. You’re probably holding both up by your ears right now. (Everyone is.)
  2. Breathe out firstA slow, empty-the-tank exhale through your mouth, like fogging up a mirror. Starting on an out-breath stops the first in-breath from being a gulp.
  3. In through your nose for 4Belly rising, about seventy percent full. Not a maximum breath.
  4. Out for 6Through pursed lips or your nose. Let the air fall out; don’t push it.
  5. Repeat six times4 + 6 = 10 seconds a breath, so six breaths is exactly one minute. If 6 seconds feels like straining, use 3 in and 5 out instead.

One minute is the minimum. If you can stay with it for two to five minutes, do. The NHS breathing exercise for stress runs for at least five, with a gentle count of 1 to 5 in and 1 to 5 out.

What if it’s a panic attack?

Breathe slowly and remind yourself it will pass. The NHS notes that most panic attacks last between 5 and 20 minutes and that, although they are frightening, they are not dangerous. Don’t force big breaths: keep the out-breath slow and longer than the in-breath.

The NHS advice for the moment itself is refreshingly simple:

  • Stay where you are, if you can.
  • Breathe slowly and deeply.
  • Remind yourself the attack will pass.
  • Focus on something peaceful and relaxing.
  • Remember it isn’t life-threatening.

If panic attacks keep coming back, see a GP or doctor. Panic disorder is a recognised condition, and a doctor can talk you through options. And if anything feels different from your usual panic, especially chest pain, treat it as an emergency first and anxiety second.

How do I ground myself with 5-4-3-2-1?

Name five things you can see, four you can touch, three you can hear, two you can smell and one you can taste. It gives your attention a concrete job in the room you’re actually in, instead of the scenario your brain is busy rehearsing. University of Rochester Medicine suggests starting with slow, deep breaths first, then working down the senses.

  1. 5 things you can seeThe blue mug. The crack in the ceiling. Be specific; it slows you down.
  2. 4 things you can touchYour sleeve, the desk edge, the floor under your feet, your phone’s cold back.
  3. 3 things you can hearTraffic, a fridge hum, your own breath.
  4. 2 things you can smellCoffee, soap. If nothing comes, name two smells you like.
  5. 1 thing you can tasteToothpaste, tea, nothing much. All answers count.

Breathing first, grounding second is a good order: the breath lowers the volume, then the naming changes the channel.

Which breathing pattern is best for calming down?

Any slow pattern with an exhale at least as long as the inhale. For a hard moment, a longer exhale (4 in, 6 out) is the simplest. If you need to stay sharp, say before speaking, box breathing keeps you calm without making you drowsy.

How do I stop feeling like this so often?

Practise when you’re calm, not only when you’re not. The NHS says you’ll get the most benefit from its breathing exercise if you do it regularly, as part of your daily routine. A 2023 Stanford study (Balban and colleagues) found that five minutes a day of breathing practice for a month improved mood more than the same time spent on mindfulness meditation, and the exhale-focused “cyclic sighing” did best.

The goal isn’t to become a breathing monk. It’s to make the 60-second version automatic, so it’s there when your brain isn’t.

  • Same time, same trigger. After your first coffee, before you open email, as the laptop lid closes.
  • One minute counts. A tiny daily habit beats a heroic weekly one.
  • Plan for missed days. You will miss some. Start again the next day without the guilt spiral.
  • Notice what works for you. Rate how you feel afterwards for a week and keep the pattern that wins.

Common questions

How long does it take for breathing to calm you down?

Give it at least a minute, about six slow breaths, before you judge it. The NHS breathing exercise for stress runs for at least five minutes, and practising daily makes the short version work better when you need it.

Should I breathe through my nose or my mouth to calm down?

The NHS suggests breathing in through your nose and out through your mouth, gently and without forcing it. If that feels awkward, nose in and nose out is fine: slow matters more than the route.

Why does a longer exhale calm you down?

Your heart rate rises slightly as you breathe in and falls as you breathe out, so a longer out-breath spends more of each minute on the slowing side. Slow breathing, under about ten breaths a minute, is linked with calmer body signals in research, though the studies are still limited.

Can I do this in a meeting without anyone noticing?

Yes. Breathe in through your nose and out slowly through your nose or slightly parted lips; nobody can see a long exhale. With an Apple Watch, ATHA can tap the rhythm on your wrist so you don’t need to look at anything.

Is ATHA a treatment for anxiety or panic attacks?

No. ATHA is a wellness app that guides breathing; it doesn’t diagnose or treat anything. If anxiety or panic attacks keep happening, see a GP or doctor. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.
