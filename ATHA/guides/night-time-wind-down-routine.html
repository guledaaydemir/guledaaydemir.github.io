A wind-down routine that takes ten minutes

By Gül Eda Aydemir, indie iOS developer · Updated 15 Oct 2026 · 6 min read

Short answer

Drop the lights half an hour before bed, spend two minutes writing down specific next steps for anything unfinished, leave your phone out of reach, then breathe slowly for five minutes once you are lying down. That's it. The steps matter less than the repetition — done nightly, the sequence itself can become the signal that the day is over.

Why do long bedtime routines fail?

Because they only fit your good evenings. Most published evening routines assume a quiet hour, a bath, a journal, a stretch sequence and a book. They are pleasant and they work — on the nights you can do them. The problem is that the nights you most need a wind-down are precisely the nights you got home at eleven, ate late, and have neither the hour nor the will.

A routine's value comes largely from repetition. An elaborate routine done nine nights then abandoned produces nothing durable. A minimal routine done five nights a week for three months can become a genuine cue. Design for your worst evening, not your best one.

What does a ten-minute wind-down look like?

Lights down, two minutes writing tomorrow’s next steps, phone out of reach, then five minutes of slow breathing in bed.

  1. Minute 0 — drop the lightsOverhead lights off, lamps only, roughly thirty minutes before you intend to sleep. It is an easy change that costs nothing, and the Sleep Foundation suggests keeping your lights dim as you get ready for bed.
  2. Minutes 1–3 — close the loopsWrite specific next steps for anything unfinished. "Email Anna the invoice at 9am", not "invoice". Specific actions release the loop; vague reminders keep it open. In a small sleep-lab study, people who spent five minutes writing a specific to-do list fell asleep faster than people who wrote about tasks they had already finished (Scullin et al.).
  3. Minutes 3–5 — put the phone somewhere elseIdeally charging in another room. If that's unrealistic, across the room is still a meaningful barrier. Not only the light: mostly the content. One notification reopens everything you just closed.
  4. Minutes 5–10 — breathe in bedLying in your sleeping position, run a slow pattern with the exhale longer than the inhale. Four in, eight out, or 4-7-8 if holds suit you. Five minutes, without checking whether it's working.

How do I make a wind-down routine stick?

Attach it to something you already do, keep the order identical and have a two-step version ready for bad nights.

  • Anchor it to something you already do. Brushing your teeth is the usual anchor. New habits tend to stick better when attached to an existing habit.
  • Keep the sequence identical. Same order, every night. The order is what becomes the cue; varying it dilutes the signal.
  • Have a shortened version ready. On bad nights: lights low, five minutes of breathing. Two steps instead of four. Missing entirely breaks the chain — a reduced version doesn't.
  • Don't measure it straight away. Tracking whether your sleep has improved is itself a form of vigilance. Give it a couple of weeks before judging.

What can I leave out?

Anything that makes the routine longer without making it easier to repeat. Things commonly recommended that are worth deprioritising if time is tight:

  • Blue-light glasses. Simply using less light is the easier, free first step.
  • Sleep tracking. For anyone anxious about sleep, a nightly score can make things worse rather than better.
  • Long journalling. Useful in its own right, but at bedtime it can reopen the loops you were trying to close. Keep it to next steps.
  • Late exercise as a wind-down. Regular exercise during the day is one of the NHS’s sleep tips, but it is not a wind-down step.

What can't a wind-down routine fix?

It can’t make up for an irregular wake time, a late coffee, an alcohol nightcap or a bedroom that is too warm. If changing your habits hasn't helped, you've had trouble sleeping for months, or it is affecting your daily life, the NHS suggests seeing a GP rather than adding more steps. A GP may offer cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), and the NHS notes that GPs now rarely prescribe sleeping pills.

Common questions

How long should a bedtime routine be?

Short enough that you'll still do it when you get home late and tired. Ten minutes is realistic. A simple routine you keep doing is worth more than an elaborate one you drop after a week.

Does a bedtime routine actually improve sleep?

The individual steps have modest effects. Repetition is what matters — the same short sequence nightly can become a cue: a familiar signal that the day is over.

What if I get home too late?

Do the shortened version: lights low, five minutes of slow breathing in bed. Preserving the sequence on hard nights is what protects the cue.
