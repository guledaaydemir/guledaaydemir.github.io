A wind-down routine that takes ten minutes

Short answer

Drop the lights half an hour before bed, spend two minutes writing down specific next steps for anything unfinished, leave your phone out of reach, then breathe slowly for five minutes once you are lying down. That's it. The steps matter less than the repetition — done nightly, the sequence itself becomes the signal that the day is over.

Why long routines fail

Most published evening routines assume a quiet hour, a bath, a journal, a stretch sequence and a book. They are pleasant and they work — on the nights you can do them. The problem is that the nights you most need a wind-down are precisely the nights you got home at eleven, ate late, and have neither the hour nor the will.

A routine's value comes almost entirely from repetition. An elaborate routine done nine nights then abandoned produces nothing durable. A minimal routine done five nights a week for three months becomes a genuine cue. Design for your worst evening, not your best one.

The ten minutes

  1. Minute 0 — drop the lightsOverhead lights off, lamps only, roughly thirty minutes before you intend to sleep. Bright overhead light late in the evening is the single environmental factor most worth changing, and it costs nothing.
  2. Minutes 1–3 — close the loopsWrite specific next steps for anything unfinished. "Email Anna the invoice at 9am", not "invoice". Specific actions release the loop; vague reminders keep it open.
  3. Minutes 3–5 — put the phone somewhere elseIdeally charging in another room. If that's unrealistic, across the room is still a meaningful barrier. The issue is not blue light, it's that one notification reopens everything you just closed.
  4. Minutes 5–10 — breathe in bedLying in your sleeping position, run a slow pattern with the exhale longer than the inhale. Four in, eight out, or 4-7-8 if holds suit you. Five minutes, without checking whether it's working.

Set an ATHA routine for your usual bedtime. A gentle reminder, then a guided session — no decisions to make when you're already tired.

Make it stick

  • Anchor it to something you already do. Brushing your teeth is the usual anchor. New habits attach to existing ones far more reliably than to clock times.
  • Keep the sequence identical. Same order, every night. The order is what becomes the cue; varying it dilutes the signal.
  • Have a shortened version ready. On bad nights: lights low, five minutes of breathing. Two steps instead of four. Missing entirely breaks the chain — a reduced version doesn't.
  • Don't measure it for the first fortnight. Tracking whether your sleep has improved is itself a form of vigilance. Run it for two weeks before judging.

What to leave out

Things commonly recommended that are worth deprioritising if time is tight:

  • Blue-light glasses. The evidence is thin compared with simply using less light overall.
  • Sleep tracking. For anyone anxious about sleep, a nightly score frequently makes things worse rather than better.
  • Long journalling. Useful in its own right, but at bedtime it can reopen the loops you were trying to close. Keep it to next steps.
  • Late exercise as a wind-down. Exercise helps sleep enormously — earlier in the day. It is not a wind-down step.

What this routine can't fix

An inconsistent wake time will undo a perfect evening routine. So will a 4pm coffee, an alcohol nightcap, or a bedroom that is too warm. If the routine is in place for a month and sleep is still difficult three or more nights a week, that is the point to talk to a doctor rather than add more steps — chronic insomnia has effective treatment, and CBT-I in particular outperforms anything you can assemble yourself.

Common questions

How long should a bedtime routine be?

Short enough that you'll still do it when you get home late and tired. Ten minutes is realistic. Four nights a week of a simple routine beats nine days of an elaborate one.

Does a bedtime routine actually improve sleep?

The individual steps have modest effects. Repetition is what matters — the same short sequence nightly becomes a cue, and your body starts preparing before you reach the bed.

What if I get home too late?

Do the shortened version: lights low, five minutes of slow breathing in bed. Preserving the sequence on hard nights is what protects the cue.
