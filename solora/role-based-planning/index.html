Role-based planning: balance work, family and yourself without a longer list

Most weeks don't fall apart because you're lazy. They fall apart because one role, usually work, quietly eats the others. Role-based planning gives every part of your life a seat at the table, with small weekly targets instead of impossible equal hours.

GABy Gül Eda Aydemir, indie iOS developer·Updated 15 Oct 2026·8 min read

Quick answer

To balance work, family and yourself, plan by role instead of by list. Write down the 3–6 roles you actually live (for example Work, Parent, Partner, Health), give each one a small weekly target instead of chasing equal hours, and check once a week which role fell behind. Then let one of tomorrow's three priorities go to that role.

  • Roles: 3–6, named in your own words.
  • Targets: small and weekly (“Partner 2”, not “10 hours”).
  • Check: 5 minutes on Sunday, one question: which role got squeezed?
  • Action: one small thing for that role on Monday's Big 3.

What is role-based planning?

Role-based planning means organizing your week around the roles you play, such as employee, parent, partner, friend or athlete, rather than around one long to-do list. Each role gets its own small goals, so you can see at a glance when one part of your life is getting everything and another is getting nothing.

The idea comes from Stephen Covey's work on “putting first things first”: FranklinCovey describes it as assessing our roles and goals and protecting time for the Big Rocks every week. Covey's version is a weekly paper ritual. This guide is a lighter, daily version that fits on a phone and survives a busy week.

Why it helps: a single list ranks everything by urgency, and work almost always wins urgency contests. Family dinners, your own health and side projects rarely have deadlines, so they slide. Giving them a role makes them visible.

How do I choose my roles?

Pick the 3–6 roles that take most of your time or matter most to you, name them in your own words, and make sure one of them is for you. Fewer is better: if two roles always get the same tasks, merge them.

  1. List who you are this season.

    Not forever, just the next few months. “Thesis writer” might only last a semester. That's fine.

  2. Keep it to 3–6.

    More than six and the weekly check turns into homework.

  3. Add one role for yourself.

    Health, Wellness, Mind & Spirit, Hobbyist: whatever you'd call the part of your week that's only yours. It's the role most likely to disappear.

  4. Name them so they mean something.

    “Dad”, “Studio”, “Body” are fine. You'll see these names every day.

Why weekly targets work better than equal hours

Equal hours across roles is impossible and makes you feel like you're always failing. A small weekly target, like “Partner: 2 things this week”, is achievable, easy to count and flexible about which day it happens.

Think of targets as minimums, not quotas. Work might get 15 things done in a week and Partner only 2, and that week can still be balanced, because the 2 that mattered happened.

Start lower than you think. A target you hit four weeks in a row beats an ambitious one you miss every week. You can raise it later.

How do I know which role I'm neglecting?

Once a week, compare what each role actually got with its target. The role furthest behind is your neglected role, and next week it gets one small, specific action. That's the whole check; it takes about five minutes.

  1. Count.

    For each role: how many things got done this week vs the target?

  2. Spot the gap.

    Which role is furthest behind? Usually it's obvious before you finish counting.

  3. Ask why, briefly.

    Was it a heavy week (fine) or has this role been last for three weeks (a pattern)?

  4. Pick one small action.

    Not “be a better friend”. “Text Ece about Saturday.” Put it on Monday's Big 3.

  5. Adjust if needed.

    If a target keeps failing for good reasons (exam month, a launch, a newborn), lower it for now. Seasons change; targets should too.

How do roles turn into today's plan?

Roles shape the week; the Big 3 shapes the day. Each morning or evening, pick three priorities, and when a role is behind its target, let one of the three come from that role.

You don't need every role every day. A good week often looks like this: work takes two slots most days, and the third slot rotates between family, health and home. Read more in the Big 3 method.

Role-based planning FAQ

How many roles should I have?

Three to six. Fewer than three and you lose the balance view; more than six and the weekly check becomes a chore. If two roles always share the same tasks, merge them. Solora's free plan includes 4 roles, which is a good starting set; Premium allows more.

Is role-based planning the same as “life areas” or a wheel of life?

They're close cousins. Life areas (health, career, finance…) describe topics; roles describe who you are being (a parent, a teammate, a friend). Roles tend to feel more personal and make it easier to notice when a relationship, not just a topic, is being neglected.

What if work genuinely needs all my time this month?

Then lower the other targets on purpose, for example Partner 1 and Wellness 2, and write down when the busy season ends. The aim isn't perfect balance; it's making the trade-off a decision rather than an accident.

Can my partner and I share roles or lists in Solora?

No. Solora is a personal planner: there's no account, no cloud sync and no shared lists, so each person plans on their own device. For a shared shopping or chores list, use a separate shared-list app alongside it.

Do I need an app for role-based planning?

No. A sheet of paper with your roles, a weekly target per role and a daily list of three works. An app mainly saves you the counting, keeps unfinished items from getting lost, and puts the weekly numbers in front of you.
