For low-energy days

What to do on a low-energy day (and still feel you moved forward)

Some days the tank is just low. The goal on those days isn't to push through your normal plan. It's to shrink the plan on purpose, so tomorrow-you isn't left with a pile of guilt.

GABy Gül Eda Aydemir, indie iOS developer·Updated 15 Oct 2026·8 min read

Quick answer

On a low-energy day, lower the bar on purpose: pick one small win, do the easier version of your habits (a 5-minute stretch instead of a workout), move the rest to tomorrow without guilt, and protect your streak honestly. If you've felt exhausted for weeks without knowing why, talk to a doctor. A planner isn't treatment.

  • Today's plan: 1 priority, not 3.
  • Habits: the smallest version that still counts.
  • Everything else: a decision (tomorrow, smaller, later or gone), not a guilt pile.

What should I do on a low-energy day?

Shrink the day to fit the energy you have. Choose one small win you can finish, swap your habits for their easier versions, and consciously move everything else. A small day you finish beats a normal day you abandon at 11am.

  1. Name it.

    “Today is a low day.” Saying it out loud stops you from measuring yourself against a normal-day plan.

  2. Pick one small win.

    The smallest thing that would make tonight feel a little better: reply to the one urgent email, take the bins out, send the form.

  3. Switch habits to their easier versions.

    A 5-minute stretch instead of a 30-minute workout. Two pages instead of a chapter. It still counts.

  4. Move the rest, on purpose.

    Tomorrow, a specific later day, or drop it. Decide once, then stop thinking about it.

  5. Stop when the win is done.

    Anything extra is a bonus, not the new bar.

Why does one small win work better than a full to-do list?

Because finishing something small changes how the rest of the day feels, while staring at a full list on an empty tank mostly produces guilt. One win is a plan you can actually complete.

The trick is to pick a win that is small and real. “Clean the house” isn't small. “Load the dishwasher” is. “Work on the project” isn't real. “Write the subject line of the update” is. If you finish it and feel like doing more, great. If not, you still kept your promise to yourself.

This is the same idea as the Big 3 method, turned down to 1 for the day.

The Easier Version menu: 16 habits, scaled down

An easier version is a pre-decided, smaller form of a habit that you're allowed to do on bad days without breaking the chain. Decide it in advance, on a good day, so a tired brain doesn't have to negotiate.

Two rules make easier versions work:

  • Write it down before you need it. The decision is made on a good day, not negotiated on a bad one.
  • It must be tiny but real. Small enough to do while tired, real enough that it's still the same habit.

Once you've started, you may keep going. If not, it still counts.

How do I protect a habit streak without lying to myself?

Pick your streak rule before life happens. A strict streak breaks on any miss; a flexible one forgives a single missed day but not two in a row; a shielded one lets you protect a limited number of days, each with an honest reason like being sick or travelling.

Three streak rules
RuleHow it worksGood for
StrictAny missed day breaks the streakTiny daily habits you never want to skip (vitamins, making the bed)
FlexibleOne missed day is forgiven; two in a row breaks it (“never miss twice”)Most habits, especially exercise, reading and study
ShieldedProtect a day with a reason, within a small allowance per week or monthHabits that hit predictable bumps: sleep on time, routines while travelling

Honesty is the point of a shield. Writing “Sick day” or “Travel” keeps the record true, and the allowance stops it from quietly becoming “every day”. When the bump is over, the streak is still there and so is your motivation to keep it.

How do I move tasks without feeling guilty?

Name what got in the way, then fix the task instead of blaming yourself. Most missed tasks fail for a handful of reasons, and each reason has an obvious fix.

What got in the way, and what to do about it
ReasonTry this
Too tiredMake it easier, or move it to a higher-energy slot
Too bigBreak it into smaller steps; do only the first
Too vagueRewrite it as the first physical action
No timeMove it to a specific day, not just “later”
Waiting for someoneSend one nudge, then park it until they reply
Felt blockedDo five minutes of it, or talk it through with someone
Not important nowLet it go. That's a decision, not a failure

When is it more than a low day?

If tiredness lasts for weeks, you can't explain it, or it's getting in the way of daily life, it's worth talking to a GP or doctor rather than adjusting your planner. That's the NHS's advice too.

The NHS lists common causes of tiredness, from not getting enough sleep to stress, infections and some medicines, and its self-help tips for fatigue include eating regularly, keeping a regular sleep routine, cutting down on caffeine and alcohol, and staying hydrated. A planner can make a hard week lighter to carry. It can't diagnose or treat anything, and nothing on this page is medical advice.

If evenings are when everything feels heaviest, a short wind-down can help you settle: see ATHA's ten-minute wind-down routine.

Low-energy day FAQ

Is it OK to skip habits on bad days?

Yes, and it's better to plan for it than pretend it won't happen. Decide in advance which habits get an easier version, which can be shielded with a reason, and which can simply wait. A missed day you planned for doesn't break your motivation the way a surprise failure does.

Does doing the easier version still count?

In your own tracking, it should: the habit is about showing up, not about hitting the maximum every day. In Solora, a day logged with the easier version (from the Premium Light Mode widget) counts as completed for your streak, and the habit's history marks that you used the easier version.

Should I still do my Big 3 on a low-energy day?

Shrink it to one. Pick the single most important thing, make it small enough to finish, and move the other two on purpose. If your planner lets you choose 1 daily priority instead of 3, low days are exactly what that setting is for.

How do I know if I'm burnt out rather than just tired?

A planner can't tell you that, and neither can this page. If exhaustion keeps coming back, lasts for weeks, or comes with changes in mood, sleep or appetite, talk to a doctor or a mental health professional.
