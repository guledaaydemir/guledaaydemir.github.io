The çırak's dream

A tea house in Üsküdar, an usta at the stove, a young apprentice with a red fez, and a kumbara that fills up one evening at a time.

A morning in Üsküdar

The shutters go up, the stove is lit and the double teapot begins to sing. Hasan Usta lays out the tulip-shaped glasses on their saucers and the first regulars take their usual seats: the okey players by the window, the old friends who argue about everything, the man who is always in a hurry.

Between them runs the çırak: the apprentice of the tea house. He takes the orders, calls them out to the usta, swings the hanging tray from table to table, collects the bills and wipes every table until it sparkles. That çırak is you.

Who is the çırak?

In Turkish trades a çırak learns by working beside an usta, a master. In the tea house that means learning to carry six glasses without spilling a drop, to know who takes their tea strong and who takes it light, and to keep the peace when an okey game turns loud.

In HANE the çırak grows with you: he gets quicker on his feet, better with the cloth and able to carry more, and in time waiters join him on the floor. But some jobs stay his: seeing out the cat that slipped in, and stepping between two regulars before a quarrel boils over.

The dream: a car of his own

Every evening, after the last guest has gone, the usta counts the day and pays the çırak his wage, 15 jeton, plus half of the day's tips. The çırak drops it all into his kumbara, his piggy bank, and dreams.

He is dreaming of a car. It costs 10,000 jeton, which is hundreds of evenings of wages and tips. In the game the dream has its own page, Çırağın Hayali, which opens after your third day: a painting of the çırak with his kumbara, and a road from the kumbara to the car with him walking along it as far as his savings reach.

The start of the road is stretched, so even the first evenings move him visibly. Flags stand at 1, 5, 10, 25, 50 and 75 percent, and passing one is announced in the evening. The page also shows what went in last night and over the last seven days.

From one room to tea houses across Türkiye

The first tea house in Üsküdar is a small room. From there the map of Türkiye opens up: Kadıköy, Beşiktaş, Bursa, İzmir, Ankara, Eskişehir and Trabzon, each with its own usta and its own floor plan. Some are long, some turn a corner, and two have a second storey with stairs that everyone climbs.

Every opening starts the Turkish way, with a red ribbon across the door. Swipe through it like scissors, the confetti flies and the neighbours call out "Hayırlı olsun!", may it bring good fortune.

Why a tea house?

Because the neighbourhood tea house is where so much of Turkish street life happens: news is shared, games are won and lost, deals are made and friendships go back decades, all over glasses of tea that cost almost nothing. HANE tries to capture that warmth: hand-painted characters, an old İstanbul street with plane trees, lamps, a simit cart and dolmuş minibuses going by, and a room that always smells of fresh tea.
