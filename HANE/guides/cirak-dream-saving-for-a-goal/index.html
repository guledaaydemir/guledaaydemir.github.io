The çırak's dream: what a game piggy bank teaches about saving for a goal

By Gül Eda Aydemir · Updated November 16, 2026 · 7 min read

Short answer

In HANE the çırak saves for a car that costs 10,000 jeton: every evening his 15-jeton wage and half of the day's tips go into his kumbara (piggy bank), and a road with flags at 1, 5, 10, 25, 50 and 75 percent shows how far he has come. The design mirrors what research on goals suggests: a concrete goal, small automatic deposits and visible milestones help people keep going.

Key points

  • The çırak's car costs 10,000 jeton; each evening adds his 15-jeton wage plus half the day's tips.
  • With 70 jeton of tips a day he saves 50 an evening and reaches the car after 200 evenings.
  • People tend to speed up as a goal gets closer: the goal-gradient effect (Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng, 2006).
  • Close early milestones, visible progress and a seven-day total keep a long goal from feeling endless.
  • This is general habit-building, not financial or investment advice.

What is the çırak's dream in HANE?

The çırak, the young apprentice you play in HANE, dreams of a car of his own that costs 10,000 jeton (the game's coins). Every evening the usta, his master, pays him a wage of 15 jeton, and half of the day's tips go to him as well. Both drop straight into his kumbara, the Turkish word for a piggy bank or money box.

After the third day, a page called Çırağın Hayali (The Çırak's Dream) opens before the next day starts. It shows a painting of the çırak dreaming of the car, and a road from his kumbara to the car, with the çırak walking along it as far as his savings reach. Flags stand at 1, 5, 10, 25, 50 and 75 percent. The page also shows what is saved, what is still to save, and what went in last evening and over the last seven days. The start of the road is stretched, so even the first evenings visibly move him, and passing a flag is announced in the evening. From then on, the car button (Hayal / Dream) over the scene and the kumbara line of the day report open the page again.

It is a small side story in a tea-house management game, but it rests on ideas that also help real people save toward a goal. Here is how it works, and what research says about each idea.

How long does it take the çırak to save 10,000 jeton?

It depends on the tips. Every evening adds 15 jeton plus half the day's tips, so the arithmetic is simple. The tip amounts below are examples to show the sums, not typical values from the game:

015 + 0 = 1510,000 ÷ 15 = 666.7 → 667
3015 + 15 = 3010,000 ÷ 30 = 333.3 → 334
7015 + 35 = 5010,000 ÷ 50 = 200
13015 + 65 = 8010,000 ÷ 80 = 125

Take the 50-jeton evening. The flags fall at 100, 500, 1,000, 2,500, 5,000 and 7,500 jeton, which means after 2, 10, 20, 50, 100 and 150 evenings, and the car comes after 200. The first three flags arrive within 20 evenings; after that they are 30 to 50 evenings apart. A game day lasts about four minutes, so 200 days is roughly 800 minutes: a little over 13 hours of service.

Two lessons sit in that table. Small, steady amounts add up, but slowly. And raising the daily amount shortens the road dramatically: going from 15 to 50 jeton an evening cuts the wait from 667 evenings to 200.

Why does a concrete, visible goal help you save?

"Save more" is hard to act on; "a red car for 10,000" is not. The çırak's goal has a picture, a price and a distance, and the dream page puts all three in front of him.

Research points the same way. In a field study with construction workers in rural India, Dilip Soman and Amar Cheema (2011) set aside part of each week's wages in sealed envelopes. Households saved more when the envelopes carried pictures of their children, a visual reminder of what the money was for, and also when the sum was split across two envelopes instead of one. A game is a very different setting, but the lesson carries over: give your goal a name, a picture and a price.

Why do small automatic deposits work?

The çırak never has to decide whether to save tonight. His wage and his share of the tips go into the kumbara on their own, every evening, on good days and slow ones. That is the heart of the design: saving is the default, not a fresh decision.

Defaults are powerful. Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea (2001) studied a large US company that switched its retirement savings plan from opt-in to automatic enrolment. Participation rose markedly, and many employees simply kept the default contribution rate. The inertia that stops people from starting can also keep them going once saving happens by itself. As a habit, a small amount that moves automatically is often easier to keep up than a larger one that waits for the right mood.

What is the goal-gradient effect?

The goal-gradient effect is the tendency to put in more effort the closer a goal gets. The idea goes back to the psychologist Clark Hull's goal-gradient hypothesis of 1932. In 2006, Ran Kivetz, Oleg Urminsky and Yuhuang Zheng tested it with people in "The Goal-Gradient Hypothesis Resurrected", published in the Journal of Marketing Research.

Their best-known study ran at a café at Columbia University with a "buy ten coffees, get one free" stamp card. As members collected stamps, they came back sooner: on average, the time between purchases shrank by about 20%, or roughly 0.7 days, over the course of the card. In another experiment, customers given a 12-stamp card with two "bonus" stamps already filled in completed the ten required purchases faster than customers with a plain 10-stamp card (12.7 versus 15.6 days on average), although both groups needed exactly ten coffees. After earning a free coffee, members slowed down, then sped up again as the next reward came closer.

The çırak's flags use the same mechanism. Instead of one distant goal, there is always a nearer one. And because motivation dips after each reward, the next flag is already waiting when one is passed.

Why make early progress visible?

At the start of a big goal, real progress looks like nothing: 100 jeton is 1% of a car, and that is when it is easiest to lose heart. HANE stretches the start of the road, so the çırak visibly walks forward after his first evenings, and it puts the first flags close together, at 1, 5 and 10 percent.

A related study by Joseph Nunes and Xavier Drèze (2006) gave car-wash customers loyalty cards that needed eight paid washes. Some cards had eight empty spaces; others had ten spaces with two already stamped. The head-start cards were completed more often: about 34% against about 19%, as the results are usually reported. In HANE nothing is given away (the progress is real, only drawn larger at the start), but the principle is similar: progress you can see keeps you going. For a goal of your own, choose a first milestone you can reach within days.

Why track the last seven days?

Whenever you open it, the dream page shows two numbers: what went in last evening, and what went in over the last seven days. One evening says little, because some days are simply quieter than others. Seven days smooth out the bumps and show the real pace. A weekly total also makes the useful question easy to ask, "Am I going faster or slower than last week?", without letting one bad day discourage you.

The same works for any goal: look at it once a week rather than every hour. For more ideas in that spirit, see our seven productivity lessons from a virtual tea house.

The usta's tip

Set up your own goal the HANE way: one picture, one price, flags at 1, 5, 10, 25, 50 and 75 percent, and a seven-day total you check once a week.

Is the kumbara a Turkish tradition?

Yes. Kumbara simply means a money box, and in Türkiye it has a long history. Türkiye İş Bankası began handing out kumbaras in 1928. In 2020 the bank's museum told their story in a virtual exhibition, "Evinizin Kasası İş Bankası Kumbarası" (Your home's safe: the İş Bankası kumbara), showing the classic steel model alongside many other shapes and colours. Turkish schools also mark Tutum, Yatırım ve Türk Malları Haftası (Thrift, Investment and Turkish Goods Week) every year from 12 to 18 December. When the çırak drops his coins into a kumbara, he is following a familiar Turkish habit.

Is this financial advice?

No. The çırak's kumbara is a game mechanic with game coins, and nothing here is financial or investment advice for anyone's situation. What the dream page models is a habit: a clear goal, small automatic deposits, milestones along the way and a weekly look at your pace. For decisions about real money, speak to a qualified, independent adviser.

When does the çırak's dream page open?

What is the goal-gradient effect?

It is the tendency to put in more effort as a goal gets closer. In a 2006 study by Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng, members of a café's buy-ten-get-one-free coffee card bought coffee more often as they approached the free cup.
