The çay molası method: a Turkish tea-break routine for focused work

By Gül Eda Aydemir · Updated November 16, 2026 · 7 min read

Short answer

The çay molası method is a Pomodoro-style cycle in which every break is a real Turkish tea break. You work on one task until the timer rings, then stop, pour a glass of tea and drink it away from the screen; when the glass is empty, the next focus block starts. Begin with 25 minutes of focus, take a longer pause after three or four rounds, and adjust the lengths to your work.

Key points

  • The Pomodoro Technique, created by Francesco Cirillo in the late 1980s, alternates 25-minute focus blocks with short breaks.
  • The çay molası method keeps that rhythm but turns each break into a small ritual: pour, drink, come back.
  • Studies comparing fixed breaks with self-chosen breaks point in different directions, so treat the timings as a starting point.
  • Write down your next step before every break; it makes coming back to work much easier.
  • An optional HANE game day lasts about four minutes and then waits on a card, so it fits inside one tea break.

What is the çay molası method?

The çay molası method is a simple way to work in focused rounds, with a glass of tea as the break. Çay molası means "tea break" in Turkish, and in Turkey it is an ordinary part of the working day: people stop, pour a small tulip-shaped glass of tea, talk for a few minutes and get back to it. The method borrows that habit and pairs it with the timing of the Pomodoro Technique.

The whole idea in one line: focus on one task until the timer rings, then stop for exactly one glass of tea. The glass gives the break a clear start and a clear end, and that is the hardest part of taking breaks well.

What is the Pomodoro Technique, and where does it come from?

The Pomodoro Technique was created by Francesco Cirillo, who says he developed it in the late 1980s as a university student. He challenged himself to focus for a short stretch using a kitchen timer shaped like a tomato, pomodoro in Italian, which gave the method its name.

The version most people know works like this:

  • Pick one task.
  • Work on it for 25 minutes without switching. That block is one "pomodoro".
  • Take a short break, commonly around five minutes.
  • After four pomodoros, take a longer break, often 15 to 30 minutes.

Cirillo stresses that the timer is only part of it: the technique also asks you to note interruptions instead of acting on them, plan the day and look back at where your time went. In this method the break is part of the practice itself, not a prize you earn by pushing on.

Do fixed breaks work better than breaks you choose yourself?

The evidence is mixed, which is why this method treats the timings as a starting point, not a rule.

  • In a 2023 study in the British Journal of Educational Psychology, Felicitas Biwer and colleagues followed 87 university students through a real study session. Students who took fixed breaks (6 minutes after every 24, or 3 after every 12) reported less fatigue and distraction and more concentration and motivation than students who broke off whenever they wanted. They also got about the same amount done in less time.
  • A 2026 study in Behavioral Sciences by Ali Göksu, Wisnu Wiradhany and Anique de Bruin, with 176 students doing a reading task, found the opposite for mood: self-chosen breaks led to more energy, concentration and motivation. Test results did not differ between the groups.
  • A 2022 meta-analysis of micro-breaks found that short breaks lift energy and reduce fatigue a little, and that longer breaks were linked to bigger performance gains.

The practical reading: some structure helps many people, especially with tasks that are easy to put off, but the exact numbers matter less than really stopping and really coming back. If 25 minutes cuts you off just as you get going, make the block longer.

How do you do the çay molası method, step by step?

  1. Choose one task and write its first step. Not "work on the report" but "write the report's introduction". Keep a blank "later" list beside it.
  2. Brew the tea as you sit down. With a çaydanlık, the Turkish double teapot, water boils in the bottom pot and the tea steeps in the top one; the pot can then sit on a low heat while you work. With a kettle and tea bags, simply boil the water at each break.
  3. Start a timer for your focus block. 25 minutes is a good first try. Silence notifications and put the phone out of sight.
  4. Work on that one task only. When something else comes to mind, write it on the "later" list and carry on.
  5. When the timer rings, stop and write the next step. Finish the sentence, not the chapter. One line on where to pick up is enough.
  6. Pour one glass and step away. Drink it away from the screen: at a window, on a balcony, with a colleague. If you like, play one short game round here (see below).
  7. When the glass is empty, the break is over. Rinse the glass, read your note and start the next block.
  8. After three or four rounds, take a long break. Fifteen to thirty minutes: eat, walk, talk to someone.
  9. Close the day like clearing a table. Tidy the desk, write tomorrow's first step and turn off the stove.
The usta's tip

Keep the "later" list on paper next to your glass. Each time a stray thought turns up mid-block (call the plumber, answer that email), write it down and let it go. Go through the list at the long break. Your head can stay on the task because the paper remembers for you.

How can you adapt it for deep work, studying or working from home?

Deep work

For writing, coding or design, 25 minutes can end just as you get into it. Try 50 minutes of focus and a 10-minute break, with two or three rounds before a long break. Keep the rule that the break starts when the timer rings, not when you feel like it.

Studying

Blocks of 25 minutes suit reading and revision well. Use the first minute of the break to say out loud what you just learned, then close the books until the glass is empty. As studies disagree on fixed versus free breaks, try a week of each.

Working from home

At home the risk is that a break turns into housework or the sofa. Make the tea break physical: walk to the kitchen, drink by a window, walk back. At the end of the day, wash the glass and put it away. It tells your head that work is over.

Late in the day or without caffeine

Black tea contains caffeine. If it is late or you are sensitive to it, pour a lighter glass, açık as it is called in Turkey, choose a caffeine-free herbal tea, or simply drink water. The ritual of stopping, pouring and drinking matters more than what is in the glass.

What are the most common mistakes?

  • The second glass becomes a third. One break, one glass. Refills belong to the next break.
  • Checking messages during the break. That is work in another form. Give messages their own focus block.
  • Starting too big. Ninety-minute blocks on the first day rarely last. Begin with 25 minutes and grow.
  • Treating the timer as a boss. If you are in full flow, finish the paragraph, then stop. If you ignore the timer every time, your blocks are too short.
  • Drinking at the desk. Tea sipped while you keep typing is not a break. Stand up.
  • "Just one more day" in the game. If one round keeps becoming three, leave the game out and keep the tea.

Questions people ask

How long should each focus block be?

Start with 25 minutes, the classic Pomodoro length, followed by a break of about one glass of tea. For deep work, try 50 minutes with a 10-minute break. Studies comparing fixed and self-chosen breaks disagree, so keep the length that lets you stop and return reliably.

Is the çay molası method the same as the Pomodoro Technique?

It uses the same rhythm of focused blocks and short breaks that Francesco Cirillo developed in the late 1980s. The difference is the break: instead of any five minutes, it is one glass of tea away from the screen, which gives the break a clear start and end.

Do I have to drink tea?

No. The ritual matters more than the drink. If it is late in the day or you avoid caffeine, use a light tea, a caffeine-free herbal tea or a glass of water.

Can I play a game during the break without losing the afternoon?

Yes, if the game has a clear end and you decide beforehand to play one round. A HANE day lasts about four minutes and then waits on a “Ready?” card, which makes one glass, one day an easy rule to keep.
