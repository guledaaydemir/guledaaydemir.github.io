7 productivity lessons from running a virtual Turkish tea house

By Gül Eda Aydemir · Updated November 16, 2026 · 7 min read

Short answer

Running a busy tea house, even a virtual one, rewards the same habits that make work flow: batch similar tasks, limit how much you carry at once, plan your route, delegate what others can do, maintain things before they break, keep buffers and triage interruptions. In HANE, a tea-house game for iPhone and iPad, each habit appears as a concrete mechanic, which makes the principle easy to see and remember.

Key points

  • Batch similar work: take several orders first, then call them all out at the counter in one visit.
  • Limit work in progress: HANE's tray holds two glasses at first, and that limit keeps service moving.
  • Plan your route and delegate: fewer trips save time, and slower helpers still add capacity.
  • Maintain and keep buffers: water the flowers, wipe the tables, shop before you run out and keep coins for loan instalments.
  • Triage interruptions: handle what only you can, hand off the rest, and decide before you react.

What can a tea-house game teach you about productivity?

More than you might expect, because a tea house is a small factory with guests. In HANE you are the çırak (the apprentice) in a traditional Istanbul kıraathane, a Turkish tea house. The usta (the master) brews at the counter; you do everything else. Every guest follows the same path: take the order at the table, call it out at the counter (the usta makes only what was called out), carry the glasses on your hanging tray to the table that ordered them, collect the bill and wipe the table for the next group.

A day lasts about four minutes and the room fills up fast. Here are seven lessons the game teaches by letting you feel the cost of doing things the slow way, each with a way to try it at work. New to the game? The beginner's guide covers the basics.

1. Batching: why should you group similar tasks?

In HANE: you can walk to the counter after every single order, or take orders from several tables first and call them all out in one visit. The second way saves trips, and because the usta makes only what he has heard, one clear call-out gets everything started.

The principle: switching between tasks has a cost. In a classic 2001 study, Joshua Rubinstein, David Meyer and Jeffrey Evans found that each switch costs time, more so for complex or unfamiliar tasks. Sophie Leroy later described "attention residue": part of your mind stays on the previous task, especially an unfinished one, and your work on the next one suffers.

Try it in real life

Group small, similar tasks into set slots: answer messages two or three times a day, make your calls in one sitting, run errands in one trip. One "counter visit" for each kind of work.

2. Limiting work in progress: why carry less at once?

In HANE: the hanging tray (askılı tepsi) holds only two glasses at first. Upgrades raise it to four glasses (220 jeton, the game's coins) and six (900), but early on two is the limit. That limit forces a good habit: deliver what you carry before you pick up more. Glasses waiting at the counter help no one; glasses on tables earn.

The principle: teams that use Kanban boards cap how many items can be "in progress" at once. The logic comes from queueing theory: Little's law, proved by John Little in 1961, says the average number of items in a system equals their arrival rate times the average time each spends inside. So if you can only finish so much per day, every extra task you start makes each one take longer.

Try it in real life

Choose your own tray size: two or three active tasks, and nothing new until one is delivered. A "doing" column with a hard limit, on a board or a sticky note, is enough.

3. Route planning: how much time do you lose walking?

In HANE: you drag anywhere to walk, and the çırak gets to work when he stands next to something for a moment. Every trip between counter and tables takes time that no guest pays for. A good loop does several jobs in one pass: drop the glasses, collect a bill on the way, wipe that table, take the next order nearby, then head back.

The principle: in lean thinking, needless movement is a classic form of waste.

Try it in real life

Keep your most-used tools within reach, on the desk and on screen. Plan errands as one loop, not three trips. Keep everything for one task in one window.

4. Delegation: is a slower helper still worth it?

In HANE: under Staff (Kadro) you can hire up to three waiters. They work by themselves at about half the çırak's speed, and trainings make them quicker and let them carry more. Half speed sounds poor until you notice that they keep serving while you are busy elsewhere: when the çırak goes to the market, guests wait unless waiters serve. One thing they never take over: the cat and quarrels stay yours.

The principle: delegating is not about finding someone as fast as you. It is about adding hands for work that does not need you, investing in their skills, and keeping the calls that need your judgement.

Try it in real life

Make two lists: tasks only you can do, and tasks that simply need doing. Hand off from the second list even if the result is slower at first, and spend the time you win on teaching rather than redoing.

5. Preventive maintenance: why fix things before they break?

In HANE: the flowers get thirsty once a day at a random time. Water them and nothing happens; forget them and they wilt and dent everyone's mood. After each bill the table gets wiped for the next group, and a cat that feels at home may leave a mess for the çırak to sweep up. None of these jobs earns a coin, but skipping them costs you.

The principle: small, regular upkeep is cheaper than repair.

Try it in real life

Give upkeep a fixed slot: a five-minute desk and inbox reset at the end of each day, a weekly check of backups and updates, a monthly tidy of shared folders.

6. Buffers: how much slack should you keep?

In HANE: tea, coffee, bread, kaşar cheese and oralet (an instant fruit drink) come from the market. The basket holds four packs at first (eight after a 180-jeton upgrade, twelve after a 650-jeton one), and while the çırak shops, guests wait unless waiters serve. So shop before you run out, at a quiet moment, not in the middle of a rush. Money works the same way. The bank lends up to 70% of a branch's price, but a tenth of the loan is due every day, 2% interest is added each evening (5% more when the day's instalment was not paid), and three unpaid days in a row close the branch the loan paid for. Coins kept aside for the instalment keep the doors open.

The principle: buffers absorb surprises. A little stock, a little time between meetings or an early start on a deadline turns a crisis into a small delay.

Try it in real life

Add slack on purpose: leave gaps between meetings, set your own deadline a day before the real one, and restock supplies when they run low, not when they run out.

7. Triage: which interruptions should you handle yourself?

In HANE: no day goes to plan. A cat (Mırmır, Pamuk, Aslan, Tekir or Karagöz) wanders in: you can walk up and see it out, or buy cat food and put a bowl down. Feed one often and it feels at home, gets harder to see out, climbs onto tables sooner and may leave a mess. Two regulars quarrel over a game: you can calm it yourself (it may fail), call the usta (always works, takes a while) or call the zabıta, the municipal police. Rain starts and the outdoor tables need umbrellas. The decision windows pause the game and show what each choice costs or risks.

The principle: not every interruption deserves the same response. Some need you now, some can be handed off, some can wait. Reacting to everything has a price too: in a 2008 study by Gloria Mark, Daniela Gudith and Ulrich Klocke, interrupted people made up for it by working faster, but reported more stress, frustration, time pressure and effort.

Try it in real life

When something interrupts you, pause for a second, as the game does, and sort it: do it now, hand it off, write it down for later, or let it go. Watch for the cat-food trap: quick fixes that make the same interruption come back more often.

The usta's tip

Play one day of HANE and count your trips to the counter. Play the next day calling out orders only in batches. Feeling the difference in your own hands makes the lesson stick better than reading about it.

If you play on your breaks, keep it to one day at a time; our guides to micro-breaks and casual games and the çay molası method explain why.

Questions people ask

Is HANE a productivity app?

No. HANE is a tea-house management game for iPhone and iPad. Its mechanics happen to illustrate common work principles, which makes them handy examples, but the game does not track your work or your time.

What is batching in productivity?

Batching means grouping similar tasks and doing them together, for example answering all your messages in one slot. It reduces the time and attention lost each time you switch between different kinds of work.

What is a WIP limit?

A work-in-progress limit caps how many tasks you have started but not finished. With the same capacity, fewer items in progress means each one finishes sooner, which follows from Little's law in queueing theory.

How do I decide which interruptions to handle myself?

Ask whether it needs your judgement now. If it does, handle it; if someone else can do it, hand it off; if it can wait, write it down; if it does not matter, let it go. Watch out for quick fixes that make the same interruption return.
