What is a kıraathane? Turkish coffeehouse and tea house culture explained

By Gül Eda Aydemir · Updated November 16, 2026 · 6 min read

Short answer

A kıraathane is a traditional Turkish coffeehouse, literally a “reading house”: a neighbourhood room where people drink tea and coffee, read the paper, talk and play table games such as okey and tavla. The name comes from 19th-century Istanbul coffeehouses that kept newspapers for their customers, and today it hangs over the doors of ordinary tea houses all over Türkiye.

Key points

  • Kıraathane means “reading house”: kıraat (reading) + hane (house).
  • Istanbul's first coffeehouses opened in Tahtakale in the early 1550s; 1554 is the date usually quoted.
  • Coffeehouses were where people heard the news, storytellers (meddah) and, later, read newspapers: hence the name.
  • A neighbourhood tea house runs on its regulars, an usta at the stove and a çırak with a hanging tray.

What does kıraathane mean?

A kıraathane is a traditional Turkish coffeehouse. The word joins kıraat (reading) and hane (house), so it literally means “reading house”. In everyday speech it overlaps with three other words you will hear in Türkiye, and the differences are small but real:

  • Kahvehane (or simply kahve): “coffee house”, the oldest and most general name for a neighbourhood room where people sit, talk and play games. Despite the name, the glass on most tables today holds tea.
  • Kıraathane: originally a coffeehouse that kept newspapers and journals for its customers. Today the word is painted over the doors of countless ordinary neighbourhood tea houses.
  • Çay ocağı: literally “tea hearth”, a small tea kitchen tucked into a market, an office block or a ferry pier. It brews tea and sends it out to nearby shops and offices on a hanging tray.
  • Çayhane: “tea house”, a place mainly for tea. The family-friendly çay bahçesi (tea garden) is its open-air cousin.

So every kıraathane is a kahvehane at heart, while a çay ocağı is usually a kitchen rather than a room to sit in. And when someone in Türkiye says they are “going to the kahve”, everyone knows they are most likely off to drink tea.

When did the first coffeehouses open in Istanbul?

Coffee travelled north from Yemen in the 16th century, and Istanbul's first coffeehouses opened in the early 1550s. The best-known account comes from the 17th-century Ottoman historian İbrahim Peçevi: two men, Hakem from Aleppo and Şems from Damascus, opened coffeehouses in Tahtakale, the busy market quarter near the Golden Horn, in the Islamic year 962, which corresponds to 1554–1555. Another chronicler, Âlî Mustafa Efendi, dates coffee's arrival in the city to 1553. The year you will usually see quoted, 1554, is a good shorthand rather than an exact birthday.

The idea caught on fast. Worried authorities banned coffeehouses more than once; according to the TDV Encyclopedia of Islam, Sultan Murad IV's ban of 1633 was the last. After that the state preferred to supervise and tax them, because coffee had become a valuable source of revenue. By the 19th century, Istanbul's coffeehouses numbered several thousand.

Why is a coffeehouse called a “reading house”?

Because for centuries the coffeehouse was where a neighbourhood learned things. People came to hear the news, argue about it and listen to performers. Meddah storytellers, a one-person theatre that UNESCO lists as intangible cultural heritage, brought whole cities to life with little more than a stick and a handkerchief. Karagöz shadow plays and wandering minstrels (âşık) performed there too, and epics were read aloud. Ottoman writers even called the coffeehouse a “school of wisdom” (mekteb-i irfan).

After the Tanzimat reforms, some coffeehouses made reading their main business. The first is usually given as the Sarafim Kıraathanesi, opened on Divanyolu in 1857, which kept newspapers and magazines for its customers and later sold books. It drew writers such as Namık Kemal. The name stuck, and “kıraathane” spread far beyond that one street.

Tea arrived in the same century. Migrants who settled in Istanbul from Russia and the Balkans after the 1877–1878 war helped make it popular, and in time the tulip-shaped glass of tea became the coffeehouse's everyday drink. Turkish coffee, with the coffeehouse as its natural home, was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2013 as “Turkish coffee culture and tradition”.

What is a neighbourhood tea house like today?

Walk into a mahalle (neighbourhood) kahvehane and you will usually meet the same cast. Regulars, many of them retired, keep to “their” tables and greet each other by name. Behind the counter, at the ocak (the stove), stands the man who brews, often called the usta, the master. Around him moves a young helper, the çırak, carrying glasses on an askılı tepsi: a round hanging tray held from a single handle by three arms. It swings as he walks, and not a drop is spilled.

The tables carry okey racks, tavla boards and packs of cards. Newspapers still lie about, and a television may murmur in a corner. The classic neighbourhood kahvehane has traditionally been a largely male space, while family tea gardens welcome everyone. The pace is slow: one glass becomes three, and nobody hurries.

Who are the usta and the çırak?

The pair comes from one of the oldest working traditions in Turkish life. The Ottoman craft guilds linked to the Ahi brotherhood were made up of masters (usta), journeymen (kalfa) and apprentices (çırak). An apprentice learned by working beside his master, often for years: sweeping, carrying, watching, and only slowly being trusted with the real work.

In a tea house, that means the çırak runs the glasses while the usta guards the brew. The çırak learns how dark each regular likes his tea, which table is ready for a fresh round, and when to keep quiet. One day, if all goes well, he stands at the stove himself.

What are the unwritten rules of a Turkish tea house?

Every place has its own habits, but a few are understood almost everywhere:

  • Accept the tea. A glass offered is a welcome, and turning it down too quickly can feel cold.
  • “Çaylar benden!” means “the teas are on me”. Standing a round for the table is a small, warm gesture, and the favour tends to come back another day.
  • Signal when you have had enough. In many places, laying your teaspoon across the top of the empty glass says “no more, thank you”; otherwise a fresh glass may simply appear.
  • Watch games quietly. Every okey and tavla table has its onlookers, but a good one keeps his advice to himself until the hand is over.
  • Do not rush. Nobody expects you to leave the moment your glass is empty.
The usta's tip

Visiting Türkiye? Order a tea, sit back and watch for ten minutes before you judge a kahvehane. The hanging tray moving from table to table tells you how well the place is run.

How does HANE recreate a kıraathane?

HANE is a tea-house management game for iPhone and iPad in which you play the çırak, in his red fez, of a traditional Istanbul kıraathane. The usta, with his moustache, brews at the counter; in the first branch, Üsküdar, he is Hasan Usta.

The game follows the real rhythm of the room. You drag to walk, take an order at the table, walk to the counter and call it out (the usta makes only what was called out), lift the ready glasses onto the hanging tray, which holds two at first, and set each one down at the table that ordered it. Then you collect the bill, wipe the table and welcome the next group. Regulars in flat caps fill the seats, some guests are in a hurry, and now and then a grandchild comes along. When no table is free, groups queue at the door. A game day lasts about four minutes; then comes a day report, and the game waits for you on a small “Ready?” card until you start the next one.

Okey, 52'lik and tavla tables are part of the house: the çırak hands out the sets and finds an okey table its fourth player. Quarrels over a game, wandering cats and the zabıta (municipal police) keep the day lively. Outside runs an old Istanbul street with plane trees, a simit cart and the neighbourhood fountain, and kıraathane music plays in the background. In time you open branches across Türkiye, eight in all, each with its own usta.

Questions people ask

Is a kıraathane the same as a kahvehane?

Almost. Kahvehane (coffee house) is the older, general name. Kıraathane (reading house) began in 19th-century Istanbul as the name for coffeehouses that kept newspapers for their customers; today both words describe the same kind of neighbourhood tea house.

When did the first coffeehouse open in Istanbul?

In the early 1550s. The Ottoman historian Peçevi wrote that Hakem from Aleppo and Şems from Damascus opened the first coffeehouses in Tahtakale in the Islamic year 962, which is 1554–1555. The year 1554 is the one most often quoted.

What is the difference between a çay ocağı and a çayhane?

A çay ocağı is a small tea kitchen that brews tea and carries it to nearby shops and offices, usually on a hanging tray. A çayhane is a tea house where you sit down and drink your tea on the spot.
