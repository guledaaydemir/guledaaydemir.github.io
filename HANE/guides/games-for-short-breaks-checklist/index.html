What makes a game good for a short break? A 7-point checklist

By Gül Eda Aydemir · Updated November 16, 2026 · 7 min read

Short answer

A good game for a short break is one you can finish and put down. Check seven things: it stops on its own, a session fits into a few minutes, it sends no notifications, it has no energy timers or daily log-in streaks, it has no ads or pay-to-skip offers, it works offline, and it leaves you calm rather than wound up. The last step is yours: when the round ends, put the phone down.

Key points

  • A good break game has a clear end, such as a round, a level or a day, and fits into a few minutes.
  • Notifications, energy timers and daily streaks call you back on the game's schedule; ads and pay-to-skip offers push you to keep going.
  • Offline play keeps a game break from sliding into messages and feeds, which bring your work back with them.
  • Test a new game in its first ten minutes: play one session, then try to stop.
  • No game can put the phone down for you: decide the length first and stop at the natural end.

What makes a game good for a short break?

A good game for a short break is one you can finish and put down. Before you pick one for your work breaks, run it through seven checks:

  1. It stops on its own, with rounds, levels or days that end.
  2. A session fits the break: a few minutes, not half an hour.
  3. No notifications pull you back later.
  4. No energy or wait timers and no daily log-in streaks set your schedule.
  5. No ads and no pay-to-skip pressure, such as "watch a video for a reward".
  6. It works offline, so the break doesn't drift into feeds and messages.
  7. It is calm and low-stakes, so you return to work rested, not wound up.

For each point you will find why it matters, what to check in the App Store listing or your first ten minutes of play, and how HANE does. HANE is our own game, so read those parts as a description, not a review. Whether game breaks help at all is covered in our guide to micro-breaks and casual games.

1. Does the game stop on its own?

Why it matters. A break only works if you come back from it, and when nothing ends, stopping rests on willpower alone. In a one-week field study of 46 people, Jan Ole Rixen and colleagues (2023) examined infinite scrolling, which users describe as a loop that stretches sessions they later regret; most left because of something around them, not because of the app. The authors suggest that a feed's unpredictability may act like an intermittent reward. A clear end is the stopping cue a feed lacks.

What to check. Look for rounds, levels or days in the screenshots, and notice whether the game ever waits for you.

HANE. Each day ends with a day report, then the game waits on a small "Ready?" card until you start the next one. That card is your stopping point.

2. Does one session fit inside your break?

Why it matters. Research on micro-breaks usually means pauses of ten minutes or less (Albulescu and colleagues, 2022). If a game needs twenty minutes before anything feels finished, you will either stop halfway or stretch the break.

What to check. Time your first full session. If you keep stopping mid-task, it doesn't fit your break.

HANE. A day lasts about four minutes of play (240 seconds), roughly the time it takes to drink a glass of tea.

3. Will it send notifications to pull you back?

Why it matters. A game that pings you later turns one break into several interruptions. In a 2015 study by Cary Stothart, Ainsley Mitchum and Courtney Yehnert, students who got a call or text during an attention task made more errors than those who got nothing, even without touching the phone. The evidence is mixed: in a preregistered 2019 study, Niklas Johannes and colleagues found that notifications made students feel more distracted without lowering their scores. Comparing two ordinary weeks, Kostadin Kushlev, Jason Proulx and Elizabeth Dunn (2016) found that people reported more inattention and hyperactivity with alerts on and the phone at hand than with alerts off and the phone put away.

What to check. If a game asks to send notifications on first launch, it plans to call you back. You can decline, now or later in Settings.

HANE. It has no notifications at all, so there is nothing to allow or switch off.

4. Are there energy timers or daily log-in streaks?

Why it matters. An energy bar that refills over hours, a reward that spoils if you are late or a streak that resets if you miss a day brings you back when the game says, not when your break does. José Zagal, Staffan Björk and Chris Lewis (2013) called this "playing by appointment": you arrange your day around the game. Sebastian Deterding, Jaakko Stenros and Markus Montola (2020) question such labels, saying they reflect critics' tastes more than evidence. Still, for work breaks, a game with its own schedule competes with yours.

What to check. Look for energy bars, countdowns and daily-reward calendars in the screenshots, and notice whether you are ever told to wait.

HANE. No energy or wait timers and no daily log-in rewards. Nothing happens while the app is closed: the game saves when you leave and carries on from that moment when you return.

5. Are there ads or pay-to-skip offers?

Why it matters. An ad between rounds lengthens the break and points you elsewhere. A "watch a video for a reward" offer turns the end of one round into the start of the next. Zagal and colleagues also described "pay to skip": paying to get past a challenge or a wait. Each puts a new decision in front of you just as you meant to stop.

What to check. The App Store listing says "In-App Purchases" when a game sells things inside it. Ads are harder to spot there, so count them in your first ten minutes.

HANE. No ads and no in-app purchases: nothing to buy, and no video to watch for a reward.

6. Does it work offline?

Why it matters. When a game needs the internet, messages and feeds are a swipe away, and "just checking" brings work into the break. Gloria Mark, Daniela Gudith and Ulrich Klocke (2008) found that interrupted people worked faster to compensate, but reported more stress, frustration and time pressure. Sophie Leroy (2009) described "attention residue": when you leave a task unfinished, part of your mind stays with it. An unanswered work message may do the same.

What to check. Switch on Airplane Mode and open the game: does it play? The App Privacy section of the listing shows what data it collects.

HANE. It plays fully offline, needs no account and collects no data; the save stays on your device.

7. Will you come back calm or wound up?

Why it matters. A break should leave you more rested. A tense match, a leaderboard or an unforgiving timer can wind you up more than work does. Research is thin here (lab studies of casual games mostly measure mood, not focus), so trust what you notice in yourself.

What to check. After your first session, ask: am I calmer or more restless? Do I want to go back to work, or play one more?

HANE. You play the çırak (the apprentice) in a kıraathane, a traditional Turkish tea house, carrying tea and wiping tables. A cat on a table or a quarrel over okey (a tile game for four) is a small choice, and decision windows pause the game while you decide. From a branch's sixth day, guests come more often. And this point is partly yours: if one day keeps becoming three, it is no longer a break.

How do you stop playing when the break is over?

A good game makes stopping easy, but it cannot stop for you. These habits do the rest:

  • Decide the length before you start. "One day, then back to work" is a plan; "a few minutes" is a wish.
  • End at a natural stop, such as the card or the menu, never mid-round.
  • Put the phone face down, out of reach. In Kushlev's study, people reported fewer attention problems with the phone silenced and put away.
  • Let a glass of tea be your timer: when it is empty, the break is over. The çay molası method (Turkish for "tea break") builds a work routine on that idea.
  • Move games that stretch your breaks off the home screen and keep them for evenings.
The usta's tip

Test a new game the way you would test a chair: early, before habit sets in. Play one session, then try to stop. How easy that feels on day one tells you more than any App Store listing.

Questions people ask

What kind of game is best for a work break?

One that ends on its own, fits into a few minutes and does not call you back. Look for clear rounds or days, no notifications, no energy timers or daily streaks, no ads or pay-to-skip offers, offline play and a calm pace. Then test it: play one session and see how easy it is to stop.

How do I stop playing a game after my break?

Decide the length before you start, stop at the game's natural end rather than halfway through a round, and put the phone face down and out of reach. A glass of tea makes a simple timer: when it is empty, the break is over.

How can I tell if a game works offline and has no ads?

Switch on Airplane Mode and open it: if it plays, it works offline. The App Store listing shows when a game offers in-app purchases. Ads are not always obvious from the listing, so watch your first ten minutes of play.

Does HANE have notifications, energy timers or ads?

No. HANE has no notifications, no energy or wait timers, no daily log-in rewards, no ads and no in-app purchases. It plays fully offline, and nothing happens while the app is closed. A game day lasts about four minutes, then the game waits on a “Ready?” card.
