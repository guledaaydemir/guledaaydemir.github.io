Kolay gelsin, afiyet olsun, hayırlı olsun: Turkish tea house phrases and what they mean

By Gül Eda Aydemir · Updated November 16, 2026 · 7 min read

Short answer

Kolay gelsin (“may it come easy”) is a good wish for anyone at work, afiyet olsun (“may it be health”) means “enjoy your food or drink”, and hayırlı olsun (“may it be for the good”) congratulates someone on a new shop, home or purchase. In a Turkish tea house you order with “bir çay” (one tea), choose açık (light) or koyu (strong), and “çaylar benden” means “the teas are on me”.

Key points

  • Kolay gelsin means “may it come easy”: say it to anyone who is working, and expect “sağ ol” (thanks) back.
  • Hoş geldin is answered with hoş bulduk; güle güle is said by the one who stays, not the one who leaves.
  • Afiyet olsun means “enjoy it”; eline sağlık (“health to your hand”) thanks whoever made it.
  • Order with bir çay, açık or koyu, and bir çay daha; çaylar benden means the round is on you.
  • Hayırlı olsun, hayırlı uğurlu olsun and bol kazançlar are what you say to someone opening a shop.

What do kolay gelsin, afiyet olsun and hayırlı olsun mean?

Everyday Turkish runs on short, fixed good wishes. Kolay gelsin means “may it come easy” and is said to anyone who is working. Afiyet olsun means “may it be health”: the Turkish “enjoy your meal”. Hayırlı olsun means “may it be for the good” and greets anything new. Spend ten minutes in a kıraathane, a traditional tea house, and you will hear all three.

A few letters help with the pronunciations below: ç is “ch”, ş is “sh”, c is the “j” in “jam”, ı (no dot) is a short “uh”, ğ is silent and stretches the vowel before it, and ü is the French “u”.

Greetings and goodbyes: hoş geldin, kolay gelsin, güle güle

Walk into a neighbourhood tea house and someone, often the usta (the master who brews), says hoş geldin, or hoş geldiniz to a group or to be polite. The answer is always hoş bulduk, even if you came alone; a welcome left unanswered sounds a little cold. Older regulars may greet the room with selamünaleyküm (“peace be upon you”), answered with aleykümselam.

Kolay gelsin is not “hello” or “good luck”, but a wish that someone's work goes easily: the dictionary of the Turkish Language Association (TDK) defines it as a good wish said to people doing a job. Say it to the usta at the stove, to the çırak (apprentice) clearing your table, to a shopkeeper as you walk in. It works as a greeting and as a goodbye, and the answer is sağ ol, or more politely sağ olun.

Goodbyes come in pairs too. The one who leaves says hoşça kal (hosh-chah kahl), “stay well”, or the older Allah'a ısmarladık, “we entrust you to God”. The one who stays answers güle güle. Saying güle güle on the way out is the classic learner's mistake.

Afiyet olsun and eline sağlık: phrases for food and drink

When a glass of tea or a cheese toast lands in front of you, whoever serves it will often say afiyet olsun, “enjoy it”; TDK glosses it as “eat with pleasure”. You can say it to anyone eating or drinking. A playful version is afiyet şeker olsun, “health and sugar”.

The natural thanks for a good brew is eline sağlık, “health to your hand”, said to anyone who made something by hand. Use it for the usta after a fine glass of tavşan kanı tea, or for a host after a meal (elinize sağlık is the polite or plural form). The cook replies afiyet olsun. If a glass breaks, the kind thing to say is sağlık olsun: health is what counts.

One phrase does not belong here. Şerefe (sheh-reh-feh), “to honour”, is the toast raised over rakı at a meyhane, a tavern; over tea it would sound out of place.

How do you order tea in Turkish?

Ordering is short. Catch the eye of the usta or the çırak and say bir çay (beer chai), “one tea”, or iki çay (ee-kee chai) for two. Adding lütfen (lewt-fen), “please”, is always fine; regulars simply call out Usta, bir çay! The verb for ordering is söylemek, “to say”: çay söyleyelim mi? means “shall we order tea?”

  • Açık (ah-chuhk), “open”: light tea, a little brew topped up with hot water.
  • Koyu (koh-yoo), “dark”, or demli (dem-lee), “well-steeped”: strong tea.
  • Tavşan kanı (tahv-shahn kah-nuh), “rabbit's blood”: the clear, deep red of a perfect glass.
  • Bir çay daha (beer chai dah-hah), “one more tea”: the most-used sentence in any tea house.
  • Hesap lütfen (heh-sahp lewt-fen), “the bill, please”; regulars ask borcum ne kadar?, “how much do I owe?”

Then there is çaylar benden, “the teas are on me”: you buy a round for your table, and the favour tends to come back another day. Over okey or tavla you may hear çaylar kaybedenden: at many tables, the losers pay for the tea. When you pay, the usta may answer bereket versin, “may it bring plenty”, which TDK describes as the wish of the person receiving money.

What do you say when someone opens a shop?

A new shop, a new car or a new flat all get the same answer: hayırlı olsun, “may it be for the good”. The hayır in it means good or benefit; the hayır that means “no” has its own dictionary entry, so nothing negative is meant. You also hear it for good news in general, from a new job to a paid-off loan. For an opening, people stack their wishes:

  • Hayırlı uğurlu olsun (hah-yuhr-luh oo-oor-loo ol-soon): “may it be good and lucky”. Uğur is good luck; the longer form sounds more festive.
  • Bol kazançlar (bol kah-zahnch-lar): “plenty of earnings”, for any shopkeeper, often said on the way out.
  • Bereketli olsun (beh-reh-ket-lee ol-soon): “may it be abundant”, also said over a meal or a harvest.

The owner answers sağ ol or teşekkür ederim, “thank you”. Many new businesses open with a ribbon across the door, cut in front of the neighbours.

Usta, abi, amca, çırak: how to address people in a tea house

Turkish prefers a title or a family word to a bare first name, and each one carries respect.

  • Usta (oos-tah), “master”: a title for anyone skilled in a trade, from the tea maker to a mechanic. It also follows a name: Hasan Usta. Ustam, “my master”, is warmer: an apprentice's word for his teacher, and a friendly way to hail any tradesman.
  • Abi (ah-bee), short for ağabey, “older brother”: a friendly, respectful way to address a man older than you. For a woman, abla, “older sister”.
  • Amca (ahm-jah), “uncle”: for an older man, with respect. An older woman is teyze, “aunt”.
  • Çırak (chuh-rahk), “apprentice”: the young helper learning the trade beside the usta. It names a job, not a way to call someone; the usta is more likely to call him evladım, “my child”.

In formal settings, use a first name plus Bey (Mr) or Hanım (Ms): Ahmet Bey, Ayşe Hanım. Nobody expects a visitor to get it all right; trying is what counts.

The usta's tip

On your next visit to Türkiye, say kolay gelsin to the tea maker as you sit down and eline sağlık after the first glass. Two phrases, and you are no longer just a customer.

Questions people ask

What does kolay gelsin mean?

Kolay gelsin literally means “may it come easy”. It is a good wish said to someone who is working: a shopkeeper, a waiter, the tea maker, a builder. It works as a greeting or a goodbye, and the usual reply is “sağ ol” or “sağ olun”, meaning thanks.

What do you say when someone opens a new shop in Türkiye?

Say “hayırlı olsun” (may it be for the good) or the fuller “hayırlı uğurlu olsun” (may it be good and lucky). People often add “bol kazançlar” (plenty of earnings) or “bereketli olsun” (may it be abundant). The owner usually answers “sağ ol” or “teşekkür ederim”.

How do you order tea in Turkish?

Say “bir çay” (one tea) or “iki çay” (two teas), with “lütfen” (please) if you like. Ask for “açık” for light tea or “koyu” or “demli” for strong. “Bir çay daha” means one more tea, and “hesap lütfen” asks for the bill.

What does çaylar benden mean?

“Çaylar benden” means “the teas are on me”. You say it when you pay for a round of tea for everyone at your table. It is a small, friendly gesture, and the favour usually comes back another day.

Can you learn Turkish by playing HANE?

HANE is a tea-house game, not a language course, and it does not teach grammar or pronunciation. Its text switches between Turkish and English under Settings, so you can meet everyday tea house phrases such as hoş geldin, hayırlı olsun and bir çay daha in context.
