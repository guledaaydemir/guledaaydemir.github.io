How to post a photo dump on Instagram.

Short answer: An Instagram photo dump is a carousel post of several casual photos and videos shared together. Open Instagram, tap + → Post, tap the Select Multiple icon, choose up to 20 items in the order you want them to appear, then edit, caption and share. The order you tap them is the order they post in — which is exactly where most dumps go wrong.

A photo dump is the easiest post to make and the hardest to make well. The concept is casual — a handful of unpolished photos from a week, a trip, a night out — but the execution has real friction: scrolling months of camera roll, remembering which nine you picked, selecting them in the right order inside an app that gives you no way to preview the result, and watching Instagram flatten your best shot on upload.

This guide covers the whole process: what to include, how to order it, the technical limits that actually apply in 2026, and how to stop quality loss before it happens.

What counts as a photo dump?

A photo dump is a carousel of loosely related photos posted as one unit instead of one curated hero shot. Most contain 5 to 10 slides, mix photos with the odd video, and include at least one deliberately unimpressive frame.

A photo dump is a carousel of loosely related images posted as one unit rather than one curated hero shot. The format took over because it solves a real problem: most good photos aren't good enough to carry a post alone, but together they tell a story that a single image can't.

The typical dump is somewhere between five and ten slides, mixes photos with the odd video, and includes at least one deliberately unimpressive frame — a meal, a receipt, a blurry hand. That imperfection is the point. A dump that looks art-directed stops being a dump and becomes a portfolio.

How many photos can you put in a photo dump?

Instagram allows a maximum of 20 photos or videos per carousel, with a minimum of 2. The limit was raised from 10 in mid-2024, so a lot of advice online is still out of date.

Instagram changed the carousel rules in 2024 and a lot of advice online is still out of date. Current numbers:

Third-party schedulersCapped at 10 slides by Meta's API

Two of these are worth reading twice.

The first slide sets the crop for the entire carousel. If slide one is a square and slide two is a tall portrait, slide two gets cropped to square — top and bottom cut off. This single behaviour ruins more dumps than anything else, and nothing in the Instagram interface warns you it's about to happen.

Twenty is the ceiling, not the target. Engagement falls off sharply after the first few slides. A tight eight-slide dump that people finish beats a twenty-slide dump they abandon at six, and completion rate feeds back into how widely the post gets shown.

Step 1 — How many photos should you pick?

Pick 5 to 10. Instagram permits 20, but engagement falls sharply after the first few slides, so a tight eight-slide dump that people finish beats a twenty-slide one they abandon at six.

The instinct is to include everything worth keeping. Resist it. A dump works because each slide earns its place; a dump of eighteen near-identical beach shots is a camera roll with extra steps.

A structure that consistently works:

  • Slide 1 — the hook. Your strongest image. It's the only one that appears in the feed, so it does all the work of earning a swipe.
  • Slides 2–4 — the story. The people, the place, the thing that happened.
  • Slides 5–7 — the texture. Food, hands, a sign, a mirror shot. The unglamorous frames that make it feel real.
  • Final slide — the punchline. The funny one, the blurry one, the screenshot. Give people a reason to reach the end.

If two photos say the same thing, keep the worse one. It sounds wrong and it works — the imperfect version reads as honest, and honesty is what the format trades on.

Step 2 — How do you control the order of photos?

Instagram posts photos in the order you tap them, not the order they appear in your library, and shows no preview of the result. Fix the sequence before you open Instagram, because the only in-app remedy is deselecting everything and starting over.

Here is the part that wastes the most time. When you tap Select Multiple in Instagram's picker, the app posts your photos in the order you tapped them, not in the order they appear in your library. Tap them out of sequence and your only remedy is to deselect everything and start over.

Worse, Instagram's picker shows you tiny thumbnails with no preview of how the carousel will actually swipe. You are ordering a sequence blind.

Two ways around it:

  1. Favourite as you go. Heart the photos you want during the week, then work from the Favourites album instead of scrolling months of camera roll.
  2. Build the sequence outside Instagram. Assemble and reorder the dump in a separate app, export it into a dedicated album, then select that album in Instagram and choose everything at once. The order is already correct, so there is nothing to get wrong.

Step 3 — Does Instagram reduce photo quality?

Yes. Instagram downscales anything wider than 1080 pixels and re-encodes it as a JPEG at roughly 70–80% quality, and you cannot switch that off. What you control is whether it happens once or twice.

Instagram compresses every photo you upload. You cannot turn that off. What you can control is how much work its compressor has to do.

Instagram resizes anything wider than 1080 pixels down to 1080, then re-encodes the result as a JPEG at roughly 70–80% quality. If your image has already been resized and re-compressed by another app before it gets there, you take that hit twice — and the second pass is where visible mush appears in gradients, skin tones and fine detail.

Practical steps:

  • Upload over Wi-Fi. On a weak connection Instagram may select a lower quality version.
  • Turn off data saver. In Instagram: Settings and privacy → Data usage and media quality, and enable the highest quality upload option.
  • Don't export as PNG. Instagram converts everything to JPEG internally, and a lossless-to-lossy conversion is handled more aggressively.
  • Avoid apps that re-compress. Any editor that re-saves your photo before it reaches Instagram has already spent quality you can't get back.
  • Never post a screenshot of a photo. It's a re-capture at screen resolution, and it shows.

If you edited nothing and simply want the originals posted in order, the ideal tool touches the file as little as possible — ideally not at all.

Step 4 — How do you post a carousel on Instagram?

Tap +, choose Post, tap the Select Multiple icon, tap your photos in sequence, set the crop on slide one, add a caption and share. The full version, with the parts that matter:

  1. Open the composer. Tap + at the bottom of Instagram and choose Post.
  2. Switch to multi-select. Tap the stacked-squares icon labelled Select Multiple.
  3. Choose your album. Tap the album name at the top and pick the album holding your prepared dump, so you aren't hunting through your whole library.
  4. Select in order. Tap each item in the sequence you want. Numbers appear as you go — check them before continuing.
  5. Set the crop on slide one. Whatever ratio you choose here applies to all slides. 4:5 portrait fills the most feed space.
  6. Skip the filters. Filtering a dump defeats the format. If you want cohesion, it should come from the photos, not a preset.
  7. Write the caption, then share. Keep it short — a date, a place, a joke. Dumps rarely need paragraphs.

What are the most common photo dump mistakes?

The five that cost the most: leading with a weak first slide, mixing aspect ratios, using too many slides, selecting out of order, and uploading a screenshot or an already-compressed export.

  • Leading with your weakest photo. Slide one is the entire post as far as the feed is concerned.
  • Mixing aspect ratios. Slide one's crop is imposed on everything behind it.
  • Too many slides. Completion rate matters more than volume.
  • Selecting out of order and posting anyway. You can reorder slides after publishing, but you cannot add one — so a forgotten photo means deleting and reposting.
  • Uploading a screenshot or a re-compressed export. Two rounds of compression is where "why does this look blurry" comes from.
