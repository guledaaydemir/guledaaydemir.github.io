The seamless panoramic carousel.

8 min read·Advanced

By Gül Eda Aydemir, indie iOS developer · Updated 15 Oct 2026

Quick answer: A seamless carousel is one wide image sliced into equal panels and posted in left-to-right order, so swiping reads as panning across a single picture. For three portrait panels at 1080 px each, build on a canvas of 3240 × 1350 px, cut it into three exact 1080 × 1350 pieces, and post them in order with no per-slide filter. Larger panels work too, as long as every one is the same size.

It is the most striking thing you can do with a carousel and the easiest to get slightly wrong. When it works, the swipe feels like panning a camera. When it fails, viewers see a visible jump at every slide boundary and the whole effect collapses into three photos that don't quite line up.

The difference is almost always arithmetic, not artistry.

What is a seamless Instagram carousel?

A seamless carousel is one wide image sliced into equal panels and posted in left-to-right order, so swiping reads as panning across a single continuous picture. Instagram has no panorama feature — the effect is built entirely before upload.

Instagram treats every carousel slide as an independent image. It has no concept of a panorama and provides no tool to make one. The seamless effect is an illusion built entirely on the viewer's side: because slides sit edge to edge as you swipe, precisely aligned panels read as one continuous picture.

This means the work happens entirely before upload. Instagram is not helping you and cannot warn you when something is misaligned.

What size canvas do you need for a seamless carousel?

Canvas width = 1080 × the number of panels. For the standard three-panel portrait version that is 3240 × 1350 px, sliced into three exact 1080 × 1350 pieces.

Every panel must be exactly the same size. The simplest numbers use Instagram's display width, 1080 px, per panel; a larger clean multiple such as 2160 px works the same way, because every panel is scaled down by the same amount. From that, the canvas size follows directly.

The rule

  • Canvas width = 1080 × number of panels
  • Canvas height = the height of your chosen ratio (1350 for 4:5, 1080 for 1:1)
  • Every slice exactly the same width (1080 px is simplest), no rounding, no leftovers
  • Three panels is the practical sweet spot

Three is the right default. Two barely reads as a panorama. Four or more asks a lot of the viewer, and every panel someone doesn't reach is a piece of your image nobody sees.

How do you make a seamless carousel?

Build the whole image on one wide canvas, mark where the cuts fall, slice into exact 1080 px panels, and post them in left-to-right order with no per-slide filter. The full sequence:

  1. Pick the panel count and ratio first. This is a decision you cannot change later without rebuilding the whole thing.
  2. Create the wide canvas. 3240 × 1350 for the standard three-panel portrait version.
  3. Compose across the full width. Place your photos, text and elements on the whole canvas as one image. Think of it as a single picture, not three.
  4. Mark where the cuts fall. At 1080 px and 2160 px. Keep faces, words and any focal point away from those lines.
  5. Export the full canvas once, then slice. Cut it into equal pieces (1080 px each for a 3240 px canvas). Do not export each panel separately from a repositioned canvas — that is where drift creeps in.
  6. Check the seams before posting. Put the three exports side by side and look at the joins at full size.
  7. Post in left-to-right order, all the same ratio, with no filter applied per slide. A filter applied to one panel and not another produces a colour step exactly at the seam.

Do carousel slides line up perfectly when swiping?

They line up if the panels are cut exactly, but people mostly see one panel at a time. The carousel snaps from slide to slide and few people stop halfway, so the panorama is experienced as a sequence of aligned frames rather than one continuous scroll.

Here is the detail that separates a convincing panorama from an almost-convincing one.

The carousel snaps from one slide to the next, and viewers rarely hold it halfway between two slides. In practice the illusion is experienced as a sequence of aligned frames rather than a genuinely continuous scroll.

Two consequences worth designing around:

  • Each panel should work alone. Most people see one panel at a time, at rest. A panel that is meaningless without its neighbours reads as a broken image, not part of a whole.
  • Never split a face or a word. Continuity across a seam works for landscapes, textures, skies and horizons — content where the eye forgives an interruption. It fails badly for anything the brain expects to be intact.

What images work across a seam?

Horizons, skies, coastlines, road surfaces, textures and gradients work, because the eye forgives an interruption in continuous material. Faces, hands, text and logos fail immediately.

What works across seams: horizons, coastlines, skies, wide interiors, road surfaces, repeating patterns, fabric, gradients and colour fields. Anything continuous and non-specific.

What breaks across seams: faces, hands, text, logos, small objects, and any subject with a clear single focal point that the cut happens to land on.

A reliable structure: place your three strongest focal points at the centre of each panel, and use the seam regions for connective material — sky, ground, water, negative space. Every panel then has its own subject, and the transitions carry the eye rather than interrupting it.

The other common approach abandons literal continuity entirely: three separate photos on one continuous background — a paper texture, a colour wash, a gradient — with a line of text or a graphic element flowing across all three. It gets the panoramic feel with none of the alignment risk, and it is what most creators actually post.

Why is my seamless carousel misaligned?

Almost always arithmetic: panels that aren't exactly the same size, a canvas that isn't an exact multiple of the panel width, mixed aspect ratios, or a filter on one panel and not another. A missing panel can't be fixed after posting, because you can't add a slide to a published carousel.

MistakeWhat you see
Canvas not an exact multiple of the panel widthLeftover pixels: a visible jump or repeat at each seam
Panels with different heightsInstagram crops to slide one, breaking alignment
Mixed aspect ratiosSlide one's ratio is forced on the rest — everything shifts
Uploaded out of orderThe image reads scrambled; reorder it after posting
Filter applied per slideA colour step exactly at the join
Panels with different pixel sizesEach is scaled separately, so seams drift. Keep every panel identical in size
Face or text on a cut lineThe illusion fails instantly

The missing-panel mistake deserves emphasis. Instagram posts slides in the order you tap them. Since March 2026 you can reorder slides after publishing (Social Media Today), and you can delete one, but you cannot add one. A panorama posted with panel two missing has to be deleted and reposted from scratch.

What should you use a seamless carousel for?

Travel panoramas, event recaps, before-and-after scenes, text banners and product ranges. A pattern worth trying: use the panorama for the first three slides, then continue with ordinary dump photos.

  • Travel panoramas — the original use case, and still the most effective.
  • Event recaps — a wide venue shot sliced across three, then normal photos on the remaining slides.
  • Before and after — one continuous scene, transformed across the panels.
  • Text banners — a headline spanning the full width, with each panel readable alone.
  • Product ranges — items laid out on one continuous surface, each panel centred on one product.

That hybrid gives you the visual impact of a seamless opening and the honesty of a normal dump, in one post.

Is there an app that makes seamless carousels?

Yes. An app with seamless groups derives the panel geometry once and splits layers that cross a seam correctly, so alignment cannot drift and the export order is already right.

Everything above is precise, mechanical and easy to get wrong by a few pixels — which is why DUMP has seamless groups built in.

You place photos, text and elements across a canvas that spans multiple slides, and the app handles the panel boundaries: each slide is rendered at exactly the right width, the layers that cross a seam are split correctly, and the preview shows you the swipe before you commit. Elements that span neighbouring slides stay aligned because the geometry is derived once, not recreated per panel.

Saving produces the panels in order, in a new Photos album, each rendered at 2160 px on the long edge, so in Instagram you pick the album and tap the panels in order. The sequence is already correct.

Frequently asked questions

What size should a seamless carousel be?

+

Multiply the panel width by the number of panels. With 1080 px panels in 4:5, three slides need a 3240 × 1350 px canvas, cut into three 1080 × 1350 pieces. Every panel must be exactly the same size.

Can seamless panels be larger than 1080 px?

+

Yes, as long as every panel is exactly the same size. Instagram scales each one down to 1080 px wide by the same amount, so the seams still line up. DUMP renders panels at 2160 px.

Why don't my carousel slides line up?

+

Usually the panels aren't exactly the same size, the canvas isn't an exact multiple of the panel width, slide one has a different ratio, or a filter was applied to one panel. Cut every panel from one export of the full canvas.

How many slides should a seamless panorama use?

+

Three is the practical default. Two barely reads as a panorama, and every extra panel is one more that people might not reach. You can follow it with ordinary photos in the same post.
