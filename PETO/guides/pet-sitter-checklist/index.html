Pet sitter checklist: everything to leave before you travel

By Gül Eda Aydemir · Updated November 18, 2026 · 5 min read

Go through it together before you leave and tell your vet who is caring for your pet. What should you leave for a pet sitter?

Leave everything they need to keep your pet's day the same as when you're home, plus everything they'd need if something went wrong. That comes down to three things: the routine (food, walks, play and any medication), the contacts (you, a backup and the vet) and the context (house notes, quirks and what "normal" looks like).

The same list works for a professional sitter, a neighbour popping in or a relative staying over. Put it in writing: PDSA advises leaving written instructions on how much food to give and when, and how much exercise your pet usually gets. Then walk your sitter through it in person if you can.

Contacts: you, a backup and the vet

  • Your phone number and travel dates, including times you'll be hard to reach.
  • A backup person who knows your pet and can make decisions if you can't be reached.
  • Your vet's name, phone number and address, plus the nearest emergency or out-of-hours clinic.
  • Pet insurance details, if you have a policy.

The ASPCA also recommends telling your vet when you'll be away and who can authorise decisions for your pet. Agree an emergency plan with the sitter: who they call first and what you'd want done. PDSA suggests asking what the sitter will do if they can't get hold of you.

Feeding, walks and the daily routine

Write the routine as times, not vague instructions. "Breakfast 7:30 AM, one level scoop, bowl by the back door" beats "feed in the morning".

  • Food: what, how much, when, where it's kept and the rule on treats.
  • Water: where the bowls are and how often to refresh them.
  • Walks: times, rough length, lead or harness, usual routes and anything to avoid.
  • Play and attention: favourite toys and games. Cats Protection suggests leaving favourite toys out and asking the sitter for regular play sessions.
  • Litter trays, cages and hutches: how and when to clean them, and where supplies are kept.

If your cat stays home, Cats Protection recommends that a sitter visits at least twice a day, and more often for very young or old cats or cats with health conditions. Leave enough food, litter and medication for the whole trip, plus a little extra in case you're delayed.

Medication, health notes and microchip details

If your pet takes medication, write down each one exactly as your vet prescribed it: name, amount, time and how it's given. The ASPCA suggests going over medication with the sitter in person. Ask your vet before you go what the sitter should do if a dose is missed or refused, so nobody has to guess. Keep medicines out of reach; the ASPCA recommends a pet-resistant container, because pets can mistake flavoured pills for treats.

  • Health conditions, allergies and anything your vet has asked you to watch.
  • Your pet's age and current weight.
  • Microchip number, the database it's registered with, and a recent photo in case your pet gets out.

If you keep vaccines and weight in PETO's free health record, these details are easy to copy out.

House notes and behaviour quirks

Small things trip sitters up, because nobody thinks to mention them.

  • Keys, doors and windows to keep shut, and rooms that are off-limits. Share alarm codes privately, not on a printed sheet.
  • Where the carrier, lead, towels and cleaning supplies are.
  • Hiding spots (the ASPCA suggests pointing these out for cats), escape tricks, and fears such as fireworks or the doorbell.
  • How your pet gets on with visitors, children and other animals.
  • Plants, food or objects your pet must not get into.

What "normal" looks like, and when to call the vet

A sitter can only spot a problem if they know your pet's normal. Describe a typical day: how quickly they eat, roughly how much they drink, toilet habits, energy, favourite sleeping spots and the noises they usually make. Then say plainly that if anything seems wrong, such as not eating, vomiting, unusual tiredness or trouble breathing, the sitter should call your vet (or the emergency clinic out of hours) first, then you. When in doubt, call the vet.

Make a one-page care card in PETO

If your routines are already in PETO, you don't have to retype them. PETO tip

Do a trial run. Give your sitter the card a few days early, do one feeding together and fix anything that confused them.

Reminders and family sharing while you're away

  • Your own reminders: keep them on as a quiet check-in, or switch a routine's reminders off while someone else is in charge. Routines follow your device's local clock, so after travelling, open PETO once so reminders are rebuilt.
  • Vet appointments: if a vet visit falls during your trip, add it in PETO so it gets its own reminder and shows on the calendar, and tell your sitter.
  • Family sharing (Premium): if the person caring for your pet has an iPhone, invite them to your household; joining is free. Everyone sees who fed, walked or played, and a notification like "Eda fed Buddy 🐾 — already done, no need to do it again." prevents double meals. It syncs through your own iCloud, PETO can't read it, and you can stop sharing at any time. More in the family sharing guide.
