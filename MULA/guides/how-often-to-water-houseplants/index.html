Watering

How often should you water houseplants? When the soil says so

How often should you water houseplants? When the soil is dry, not on a fixed day. Learn the finger test, pot weight, seasonal changes and plant-group patterns.

By Gül Eda Aydemir · Updated November 18, 2026 · 6 min read

Short answer

There is no single right number of days. Water a houseplant when the top 2–5 cm of potting mix feels dry and the pot feels light, then water thoroughly until it drains and empty the saucer. How long that takes depends on the plant, the pot, the light and the season, which is why watering on a fixed day so often leads to overwatering.

Key points

  • Water when the top 2–5 cm of mix is dry, not when the calendar says so.
  • Lift the pot: a dry pot feels clearly lighter than a freshly watered one.
  • Pot type, light, warmth, season and plant type all change the rhythm.
  • Water thoroughly, let it drain, and never leave the pot standing in water.
  • MULA asks how the soil feels and only logs a watering when you say it was dry.

How often should you water houseplants?

Water a houseplant when its soil is dry a little way down, not when the calendar says it is time. For most leafy houseplants that means the top 2–5 cm of potting mix feels dry and the pot feels noticeably lighter. Then water thoroughly, let the extra run out of the drainage hole, and empty the saucer.

How many days that takes differs in every home. The same monstera may want water within days in a warm, bright summer, and far less often in a cool room in winter. University of Illinois Extension notes that fixed schedules usually don’t work, and the RHS advises checking that a pot needs water before you water it.

How do you know when a plant needs water?

Your hands are the most reliable tool. Three quick checks cover almost every pot:

  • The finger test. Push a finger into the mix, a little away from the stem. Extension guides suggest feeling about 2.5 cm (UConn) to 5 cm (University of Illinois) down, roughly to your first or second knuckle. If it feels dry at that depth, it is usually time to water. If it feels cool and damp, or soil clings to your finger, wait.
  • The weight of the pot. Dry pots feel lighter than wet ones, as the RHS points out. Lift the pot right after a thorough watering to learn its “full” weight; a few days later the difference is easy to feel.
  • A stick for deep pots. In large containers, push a wooden skewer or dowel to the bottom and pull it out. University of Maryland Extension notes that moist soil will cling to it or darken it.

Leaves alone can mislead you. A wilting plant is often thirsty, but University of Illinois Extension points out that wilting can also be a symptom of overwatering, when damaged roots can no longer take up water. Touch the soil before you reach for the watering can. The guide to overwatering and underwatering signs shows how to tell the two apart.

Why watering on a fixed day leads to overwatering

A fixed day assumes the plant used the same amount of water every week. It rarely does. Growth slows in the darker months, a cloudy week uses less than a sunny one, and a pot moved away from the window dries more slowly. When you water on schedule anyway, the mix never gets the chance to dry and the roots sit in wet soil.

Roots need air as well as water. The RHS explains that when a pot stays waterlogged, water fills the air spaces in the compost and the roots can drown. Checking first takes a few seconds, and it protects the part of the plant you cannot see.

What changes how fast the soil dries?

Four things set the rhythm, and all of them shift through the year:

  • The pot. Unglazed clay is porous and dries faster than plastic or glazed pots (University of Illinois, UConn). A plant that has outgrown a small pot also needs water more often. Every pot should have a drainage hole so extra water can escape (RHS).
  • Light and warmth. More light and a warmer room usually mean faster water use (UConn). A bright window and a dim hallway are two different climates; the light guide helps you read your spots.
  • The season. Most houseplants grow actively in spring and summer and dry out faster then. In a cool room in winter, the RHS notes that many can go a week or two without water. Central heating is the exception: a pot near a radiator can dry quickly even in January.
  • The plant itself. Plants with thick or waxy leaves, or with few leaves, usually need less water (UConn). Succulents hold water in their fleshy leaves and stems, so they ask for much less.

How often to water monstera, succulents, orchids and ferns

Here are typical patterns by plant group. Treat them as tendencies, not timetables: your soil check still has the final word.

Plant groupUsual tendencyWhat to watch
Succulents and cactiWater thoroughly once the surface feels dry in spring and summer; little or no water during the winter rest, roughly November to early March in the northern hemisphere (RHS).Gritty, free-draining mix and no standing water. Too much water can lead to rot.
Tropical foliage (monstera, pothos)Water while it is growing, letting the top of the soil dry between waterings; keep just moist in winter (RHS, NYBG).The finger test at 2–5 cm. Yellowing lower leaves can mean too much water.
Moth orchids (Phalaenopsis)In bark, often about once a week in the growing season and a little less in winter; in moss, when the top feels dry (RHS, Clemson Extension).Pot weight. Keep water out of the crown and let the pot drain fully.
Ferns (e.g. Boston fern)Moist but well-drained compost: water moderately while growing and sparingly in winter (RHS).Check often; small pots in warm rooms dry fastest.

A grower’s label may differ for a particular variety, so read yours too.

Questions

How often should I water my monstera?

When the top few centimetres of potting mix feel dry, rather than on a fixed day. The RHS suggests watering monstera while it is growing and keeping it just moist in winter, so expect to water noticeably less often in the cooler, darker months. Always let the extra water drain away.

How do I know when to water my plants?

Push a finger about 2–5 cm into the potting mix. If it feels dry at that depth and the pot feels light, water thoroughly until water runs from the drainage hole. If it feels moist or soil sticks to your finger, check again in a day or two.

Is there an app that reminds me to water plants?

MULA, a calm iPhone app, sends a gentle reminder when a plant’s interval is up, but it asks how the soil feels instead of telling you to water. Only “Dry” logs a watering; “A little moist” asks again in two days and “Wet” skips the round. It is free for up to 10 plants, with no ads and no account.

Should I water houseplants less in winter?

Usually, yes. Most houseplants grow more slowly in winter and their soil stays moist for longer, especially in a cool room. Heated rooms can still dry pots quickly, so keep checking the soil rather than switching to a new fixed schedule.
