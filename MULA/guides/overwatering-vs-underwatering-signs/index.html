Overwatering vs underwatering: how to tell the signs apart and help your plant

Overwatered or underwatered? Compare the signs side by side: yellow leaves, soggy soil, root rot, crispy edges, drooping. Plus how to save an overwatered plant.

By Gül Eda Aydemir · Updated November 18, 2026 · 6 min read

Short answer

Check the soil first. An overwatered plant usually sits in soggy soil, with yellowing lower leaves and wilting that does not improve after watering; its roots may be brown, soft and smell bad. An underwatered plant has dry, light soil that may pull away from the pot, crisp brown leaf edges, and drooping that recovers soon after a thorough watering.

Key points

  • Wilting can mean too much or too little water, so touch the soil before you act.
  • Overwatering: soggy soil, yellow lower leaves, soft dark roots, fungus gnats.
  • Underwatering: a light pot, crispy brown edges, soil shrinking from the pot.
  • To rescue an overwatered plant, stop watering, check drainage and roots, and repot in fresh mix.
  • MULA cannot diagnose diseases, but its soil check helps prevent overwatering.

How can you tell if a plant is overwatered or underwatered?

Touch the soil. An overwatered plant looks unwell while its potting mix is still wet: lower leaves turn yellow, stems may soften, and it can wilt even though the pot is heavy. An underwatered plant looks unwell in dry, light soil: leaf edges turn crisp and brown, and it droops, but usually perks up within hours of a good drink.

The confusing part is that both can make a plant wilt. When roots sit in wet soil for too long, they run short of oxygen, start to die and can no longer take up water, so the plant droops as if it were thirsty (University of Maryland Extension, Clemson Extension). Watering it again only makes things worse. That is why the soil, not the leaves, is the first thing to check.

Signs of overwatering

  • Yellowing lower leaves. Wilting or yellowing of the lower and inner leaves is often the first sign of too much moisture (University of Maryland Extension). Clemson Extension notes that yellow leaves are most often a sign of overwatering.
  • Soil that stays soggy. The mix is still wet days after watering, the pot feels heavy, or water stands in the saucer or decorative outer pot.
  • Wilting in wet soil. The RHS notes that a plant wilting in wet compost can point to root damage from overwatering, or to root disease.
  • Soft, dark roots with a bad smell. Healthy roots are usually firm and white. Rotting roots are soft, brown to black, and often smell unpleasant (Chicago Botanic Garden, Iowa State University Extension).
  • Fungus gnats. Tiny dark flies running over the soil often breed in mix that stays wet. Iowa State University Extension says overwatered houseplants are their most common home, and that letting the soil dry between waterings helps.
  • Dropping leaves. Either too much or too little water can cause leaf drop (Clemson Extension), so read it together with the soil.

Signs of underwatering

  • Crispy, brown leaf edges. The RHS lists brown leaf edges as a possible sign of underwatering. Hot, dry air and salt build-up can brown leaf tips too (Clemson Extension), so check the soil before deciding.
  • Drooping that recovers after watering. A thirsty plant in dry soil often droops, then lifts again within hours of a thorough watering. If it stays limp in wet soil, think about the roots instead.
  • Soil pulling away from the pot. Very dry mix shrinks from the sides, and water runs straight down the gap into the saucer without soaking in (UConn Extension).
  • A light pot. The pot feels much lighter than after watering, and the finger test finds no moisture a few centimetres down.
  • Curling leaves and dropped buds. Ongoing dryness can bring leaf curling (Clemson Extension) and flower buds that fall before they open (RHS).

For a pot that has dried out completely, UConn Extension suggests setting it in a sink or bucket of water until air bubbles stop rising, then letting it drain well.

How to save an overwatered plant

Many overwatered plants recover if you act early. Go gently, one step at a time:

  1. Stop watering. Let the top of the mix dry before you water again, and pour away any water in the saucer or outer pot.
  2. Check the drainage. Every pot should have a drainage hole (RHS). Make sure it is not blocked, and lift the plant out of any decorative pot that holds water.
  3. Look at the roots. If the plant still wilts in wet soil after a few days, ease it out of the pot. Firm, pale roots are a good sign; soft, dark, smelly ones are rotting.
  4. Trim what has rotted. With clean scissors, cut away soft, dark roots, along with yellow or dead leaves (Chicago Botanic Garden, Clemson Extension).
  5. Repot in fresh mix. Use fresh, well-draining potting mix in a clean pot with a drainage hole, rather than the old wet soil. A pot that just fits the remaining roots usually dries more evenly than a much bigger one.
  6. Resume normal care. From now on, check the soil before every watering. New leaves are the sign the plant has settled.

If most of the roots have rotted, recovery is less likely. Chicago Botanic Garden suggests that when more than half the roots are damaged, you might consider starting again.

When yellow leaves are not about water

Yellowing and drooping have other causes too: too little light, cold draughts, pests, disease, or simply an old leaf being let go. If the soil feels right and the plant keeps declining, or you see spots, webbing, sticky residue or insects, take a clear photo and ask a local nursery, garden centre or your regional extension service. They can look at the plant and know the problems common where you live.

How a soil-first routine prevents overwatering

Most overwatering is not one big mistake. It is a habit of watering on a set day whether or not the plant used the water. A soil-first routine breaks that habit: check before every watering, water thoroughly only when the mix is dry, and let the season set the pace. Questions

Can an overwatered plant recover?

Often, yes, if you act early. Stop watering, let the top of the mix dry, make sure the pot drains and check the roots. Trim soft, dark roots and repot in fresh, well-draining mix. If more than half of the roots are damaged, recovery is less likely.

Why are the lower leaves on my plant turning yellow?

Too much water is a common cause, especially when the soil stays wet for days. Old leaves also yellow naturally, and low light, cold or pests can play a part. Check the soil first; if it is soggy, let it dry and look at the drainage.

Do fungus gnats mean I am overwatering?

Very often. Fungus gnats breed in potting mix that stays moist, and overwatered houseplants are their most common home indoors. Letting the soil dry between waterings, or repotting in fresh mix, usually reduces them.

Can MULA tell me what is wrong with my plant?

No. MULA cannot diagnose pests or diseases, and its plans are starting points, not professional advice. It helps prevent overwatering by asking how the soil feels before a watering is logged. For spots, pests or a plant that keeps declining, ask a local nursery or extension service.
