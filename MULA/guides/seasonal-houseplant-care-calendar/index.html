Seasonal care

How should houseplant care change with the seasons?

How houseplant care changes from spring to winter: when to repot, whether to fertilize in winter, how to water less, and how MULA’s four-season plans adjust.

By Gül Eda Aydemir · Updated November 18, 2026 · 6 min read

Short answer

Most houseplants grow when days are long and bright and slow down when they are short and dark, so care follows the light: in spring and summer, water more often, feed regularly and repot if needed; in autumn and winter, water less, feed little or not at all, and keep plants away from cold glass and radiators. Some plants, such as camellias, poinsettias and many orchids, keep their own calendar. MULA gives every plant a four-season plan, and with “Follow the seasons automatically” on, its reminders shift when the season changes.

Key points

  • Most houseplants grow with long, bright days and slow down in short, dark ones; watering and feeding should follow.
  • Spring is the usual time to start feeding again and to repot, once new growth appears.
  • In winter, most houseplants need less water and little or no fertilizer; check the soil before you water.
  • Camellias, poinsettias and many orchids keep their own calendar, so shape their plan around the plant.
  • In MULA, every plant has a four-season plan; a Resting season pauses feeding, and the Southern hemisphere switch reverses the seasons.

How does houseplant care change through the year?

Most houseplants follow the light. In long, bright days they grow, drink more and can use fertilizer; in short, dark days they slow down and need less water and little or no feeding. The RHS puts the main growing season for houseplants at roughly March to October in the UK.

That gives a simple rhythm to adapt to your home and to each plant:

Treat it as a starting point, not a rule: the soil is always a better guide than the calendar. The guide to how often to water houseplants explains how to read it.

Spring: when to repot houseplants and start feeding again

As the days lengthen, new leaves usually appear. That is the signal to wake the routine gently: water a little more often as the plant uses more, and start feeding again once growth has clearly resumed. Iowa State University Extension recommends fertilizing regularly in spring and summer, while plants are actively growing. Follow the dose on the label.

Spring is also the classic time to repot. Penn State Extension says the best time is spring, when a plant naturally begins its seasonal growth, and the RHS adds that plants potted on in spring can establish during the growing season. Most don’t need it every year: the RHS suggests potting on every two to three years. Signs it is time, according to Penn State, include roots growing through the drainage holes or circling the root ball. Choose a pot only slightly larger than the old one.

Summer: more water, more light, and heat to watch for

The RHS notes that with higher temperatures plants are more active and dry out faster, so the gaps between waterings usually shrink. Keep checking the soil first, though.

The RHS also warns that strong summer sun can scorch susceptible plants through glass, and that excessive heat makes plants that prefer cooler conditions dehydrate and wilt. If your plants spend the summer on a balcony, see the guide to protecting balcony plants from frost and heat.

Autumn: slowing down and bringing plants back inside

As the days shorten, growth slows, and care can slow with it: water a little less often and ease off feeding. Iowa State Extension advises stopping fertilizer in autumn and winter for plants that are no longer actively growing.

Houseplants that spent the summer outdoors should come in before the cold, not after it. Iowa State suggests bringing them inside when nights dip consistently to around 10°C (50°F), since many are damaged once nights fall to about 7°C. Check them for pests before they come in, and expect a few dropped leaves while they adjust.

Should I fertilize houseplants in winter?

For most houseplants, little or not at all. Iowa State Extension says fertilizer is generally not necessary in winter because most houseplants are not growing. Clemson’s Home & Garden Information Center notes that many indoor plants with little or no artificial light enter a resting stage in the short days and should not be fed while they rest. The RHS is slightly softer: feed no more than once a month, because light levels are too low. Plants under artificial light, and those in the next section, can be exceptions.

Winter watering is mostly patience. With less light and lower temperatures, plants grow less and need less water, says the RHS; in a cool room many can go a week or two without. Central heating dries the air, though, and pots can dry out faster in a warm room. So water by the soil, not by the date. If leaves yellow or the compost stays wet for days, the guide to signs of overwatering can help.

Mind the spot, too. The RHS says houseplants benefit from full sunlight when winter daylight is weak; the light guide helps you choose a window. It also warns that the space behind drawn curtains can get very cold, that cold draughts can shock a plant, and that radiators can brown leaf edges. Grouping plants, or standing them on a tray of moist gravel, raises the humidity around them.

Plants that keep their own calendar

Spring growth and winter rest is a useful default, but some popular plants don’t follow it.

  • Camellia. The RHS says Camellia japonica flowers in late winter and early spring, and its flowers are formed over summer, so the soil shouldn’t dry out then. It also advises not feeding after the end of July, as late feeding can cause bud drop.
  • Poinsettia. Its season is winter. Purdue Extension calls it a long-night plant: to bloom again, it needs complete darkness every night from early October to mid-December.
  • Moth orchids (Phalaenopsis). They can flower at any time of year, says the RHS. In winter it suggests bright light, slightly less water and little or no feed. A spot about 5°C cooler for a month or so can encourage a flower spike, but buds drop if the plant gets too cold.

How MULA’s four-season plans work

In MULA, every plant has a four-season plan: for spring, summer, autumn and winter, it sets how often to water and to feed, with a short tip for the season. A season can be marked Resting: “Less water and no feeding while it rests.” Feeding reminders pause then and start again on the first day of the next season that has feeding. Each plan also holds a temperature range, such as “Happy between 15 and 30°C”.

With “Follow the seasons automatically” on, reminders shift when the season changes. South of the equator, the Southern hemisphere switch in Settings reverses the seasons. Today shows the season above the headline, for example “Today · Autumn”.

The interval is never an order. When it is up, MULA asks “How does Momo’s soil feel?” and logs a watering only when you answer “Dry · I watered it”. “A little moist” asks again in two days, and “Wet” skips the round. In a cool winter room, that question matters more than any number.

New plants start with “A gentle starting plan”, and the built-in plans are starting points, not botanical rules. Viewing every plan is free; editing the season numbers is part of MULA Premium.

MULA tip

Before you change a number, let the soil check speak for a season. If you keep answering “A little moist” in winter, the plant’s winter interval could be longer.

Questions

Should I fertilize houseplants in winter?

Usually little or not at all. Most houseplants are not actively growing in winter, so Iowa State University Extension says fertilizer is generally not necessary, and the RHS suggests feeding no more than once a month. Plants under artificial light and winter-flowering plants can be exceptions; follow the label.

When is the best time to repot houseplants?

Usually in spring, when a plant begins its seasonal growth, so it can settle in during the growing season. Repot when roots grow through the drainage holes or circle the root ball, into a pot only slightly larger. The RHS suggests potting on every two to three years.

How often should I water houseplants in winter?

Less often than in summer, but there is no single number. In a cool room many houseplants can go a week or two without water, while central heating can dry pots out faster. Check the soil before you water rather than following a fixed day.
