Light

How much light does my houseplant need? Low light, bright indirect light and direct sun, explained

Low light, bright indirect or direct sun? Rough lux ranges, how windows and distance change light, signs of too little or too much, and how to measure it.

By Gül Eda Aydemir · Updated November 18, 2026 · 6 min read

Short answer

Many leafy houseplants do best in bright, indirect light: near a window with a wide view of the sky, but out of strong sun on the leaves. As a rough guide from extension services, low light is under about 1,000 lux (100 foot-candles), bright indirect light about 1,000 to 5,000 lux, high light up to about 10,000 lux and direct sun above that, though sources draw the lines differently. Stretched, leaning growth usually means too little light; pale, scorched patches usually mean too much.

Key points

  • Many leafy houseplants do best in bright, indirect light; cacti, succulents and citrus want direct sun.
  • Rough ranges: low light under about 1,000 lux, bright indirect about 1,000 to 5,000 lux, direct sun above about 10,000 lux.
  • Light drops quickly as you move back from a window, and it changes with the season.
  • Stretching and leaning point to too little light; pale, scorched patches point to too much.
  • A phone reading, including MULA’s Premium light meter, is an estimate: compare spots rather than trust one number.

How much light does a houseplant need?

Many of the leafy plants we keep indoors do best in bright, indirect light: close enough to a window to see a wide piece of sky, but out of strong sun falling on the leaves for hours. A smaller group, such as cacti, succulents and citrus, wants direct sun. Others, like snake plant, ZZ plant and peace lily, are known for coping with low light.

Light for plants is measured in lux or foot-candles; one foot-candle is about 10.8 lux. Roughly, low light is under about 100 foot-candles (around 1,000 lux), bright indirect light runs to several thousand lux, and direct sun through a window starts around 10,000 lux. Published ranges differ, so treat every number as a neighbourhood, not an address.

Low light vs bright indirect light vs direct sun

Plant labels use these words loosely. Here is what they usually mean in a room, with the rough midday ranges published by the University of Maryland Extension and UF/IFAS, converted to lux and rounded.

Light levelWhere you usually find itFoot-candlesLux (rounded)
Low lightFar from windows, or near a north-facing window25–100about 250–1,000
Medium to bright, indirectNear an east- or west-facing window, out of direct rays100–500about 1,000–5,000
High lightClose to a south- or west-facing window, some direct sun500–1,000about 5,000–10,000
Direct sunAn unshaded south-facing window or a sunroomover 1,000over about 10,000

Other services draw the lines elsewhere: Illinois Extension uses benchmarks of about 75, 150, 300 and 1,500 foot-candles for low, medium, high and direct light. Maryland Extension’s lists also hint at which plants sit where: snake plant, ZZ plant and peace lily in low light, rubber plant and dracaena in medium-bright light, cacti, succulents and citrus in direct sun. “Tolerates low light” usually means a plant copes there, not that it grows its best there.

How many lux does a monstera need?

There is no single official number. NC State Extension describes Monstera deliciosa as liking moderate brightness but not direct sunlight, with variegated plants needing more light than plain green ones. That is the medium to bright indirect band above, roughly 1,000 to 5,000 lux around midday. Kept well below it, a monstera usually grows slowly, with smaller leaves.

How window direction and distance change the light

In the northern hemisphere, a south-facing window is usually the brightest spot in a home. Nebraska Extension offers a useful comparison: if a south window gives 100%, east and west windows give about 60% and a north window about 20%. West-facing windows also tend to run warmer than east-facing ones. In the southern hemisphere, swap north and south.

Distance matters even more. Light falls off rapidly as you move away from the glass; Nebraska Extension points out that moving a plant only slightly further back cuts the light very quickly. In Illinois Extension’s rough guide, the spot right in front of a north window counts as medium light, but a few feet back it is already low light.

Curtains, insect screens, trees, buildings and the season take light away too. Winter sun is lower and the days are shorter, and Nebraska Extension notes that a plant growing well in an east window in summer may need a south window in winter.

Signs your plant is not getting enough light

Too little light shows slowly, over weeks:

  • Stretching: long, thin stems with wide gaps between the leaves, often called leggy growth.
  • Leaning toward the window, because light reaches the plant from one side.
  • Smaller new leaves and a paler colour than the older ones.
  • No flowers on plants that normally bloom.

Moving the plant closer to a window, or to a brighter one, usually helps the next leaves; stretched stems won’t shrink back, but they can often be trimmed in the right season. A plant in low light also uses water more slowly, which is one more reason to touch the soil before watering, as in the guide to how often to water houseplants.

Signs your plant is getting too much light

Too much direct sun usually shows faster. Nebraska Extension calls it sunburn: pale patches that eventually turn brown and die, often on the side facing the glass. Maryland Extension suggests protecting houseplants from strong direct sun in the summer months.

Scorched patches don’t turn green again, so move the plant back from the window or behind a sheer curtain and judge by the new leaves. When moving a plant into a much brighter spot, doing it in steps over a week or two is usually gentler. And not every brown leaf is about light: soil that stays wet can leave similar marks, so rule that out with the guide to signs of overwatering.

How to measure light for plants with your phone

UF/IFAS suggests a hand-held digital light meter is often the best option. Phone apps are handier, and UF/IFAS says they are usually accurate enough to show a change in light levels but should only be used for approximations. That is the honest way to use any phone reading: to compare spots, not to get a laboratory number.

  • Measure where the leaves will be, not on the windowsill.
  • Take readings mid-morning, at midday and in the late afternoon.
  • Measure again when the seasons change.

MULA’s light meter: an estimate in lux

MULA’s light meter is part of MULA Premium. Open it from the sun icon in Garden, or tap Measure next to the light choice when you add a plant. Hold your iPhone where the plant will live, back camera facing the window. MULA reads the camera’s exposure and turns it into an approximate value, such as “About 2,400 lux · an estimate”.

The reading is sorted into the three light choices you pick when adding a plant:

  • Low light: under about 800 lux.
  • Bright, indirect: about 800 to 10,000 lux.
  • Direct sun: above about 10,000 lux.

The bands are broad on purpose and roughly in line with the published ranges above; MULA folds medium and high light into one “Bright, indirect” band. Below the reading, “Plants that would like it here” or “Your plants in this spot” show which plants suit that light.

It is an estimate, and MULA says so: a phone camera is not a lab instrument, and light changes with the hour and the season. Take a few readings and trust the pattern more than any single number. No photo is taken or saved, and the reading happens on your iPhone. Choosing a light level by hand when you add a plant is free.

MULA tip

Take three readings in the same spot: mid-morning, midday and late afternoon. If the label jumps from “Bright, indirect” to “Direct sun” around noon, that spot gets a few hours of sun, which is worth knowing before you choose a plant for it.

Light, water and the seasons work together

As the days shorten, many houseplants slow down and need water less often, then pick up again in spring. MULA’s four-season care plans can follow that rhythm, shifting reminders when the season changes; the guide to seasonal plant care explains how. Whatever the season, the soil check still decides whether a plant gets water.

Questions

What does bright, indirect light mean?

It is a spot near a window where the plant sees plenty of sky, but the sun’s rays do not fall on its leaves for long. In extension tables it is roughly 100 to 500 foot-candles, or about 1,000 to 5,000 lux at midday, though sources draw the edges differently.

How many lux is low light for plants?

Extension tables such as the University of Maryland’s put low light at about 25 to 100 foot-candles, roughly 250 to 1,000 lux. MULA’s light meter treats readings under about 800 lux as low light. Plants such as snake plant, ZZ plant and peace lily are known for coping with it.

Can I measure light for plants with my phone?

Yes, roughly. UF/IFAS says phone apps are usually good enough to show changes in light but should be treated as approximations. MULA’s Premium light meter uses the back camera’s exposure to estimate lux, sorts the spot into low light, bright indirect or direct sun, and does not take or save a photo.

How much light does a monstera need?

NC State Extension describes Monstera deliciosa as liking moderate brightness but not direct sunlight, with variegated plants needing more light. In lux that is roughly the 1,000 to 5,000 range at midday: near a bright window, out of strong direct sun.

Is a north-facing window enough for houseplants?

In the northern hemisphere, a north window gives about a fifth of the light of a south window, according to Nebraska Extension. Right in front of it is roughly medium light, and a few feet back it drops to low light, so it suits plants that cope with lower light. In the southern hemisphere the same is true of south-facing windows.
