Frost and heat

How do you protect balcony plants from frost and heat waves?

How to protect balcony and potted plants from frost and heat waves: why pots are more exposed, what to do before a cold night or hot day, and how MULA helps.

By Gül Eda Aydemir · Updated November 19, 2026 · 6 min read

Short answer

Potted plants feel frost and heat sooner than plants in the ground, because their roots are more exposed. Before a frosty night, group pots against a sheltered house wall, wrap the pots, cover tender plants with fleece or bring them under cover, and avoid watering; in a heat wave, water early, shade pots from the midday sun, mulch the compost and keep dark or metal pots out of full sun. Why are balcony plants in pots more at risk from frost and heat?

Because their roots have nowhere to hide. In the ground, roots sit deep in soil that buffers the cold; in a pot, only the compost and the pot wall stand between them and the air. The RHS notes that container plants are more susceptible to cold because their roots are more exposed than those buried deep in the earth, and Iowa State University Extension points out that the roots of most trees and shrubs are far less cold-hardy than their stems. A plant that copes with a frost in a border can be hurt by the same night on a balcony.

Heat works the same way. N.C. Cooperative Extension warns that black or dark containers in the sun can heat up fast, with soil 20 to 30°F (roughly 11 to 17°C) warmer than the surrounding air, and that roots are far more heat-sensitive than leaves. So in both seasons the answer is similar: protect the pot, not only the plant.

How to protect balcony plants on a frosty night

Act when frost is first forecast: the RHS says protective wrappings should go on at the first sign of frosts.

  • Move pots against the house wall and group them. The RHS says grouping containers close together near a house wall in winter helps protect roots from the cold. The base of a sunny wall (south-facing in the northern hemisphere) is one of the warmer places.
  • Wrap the pot, not just the plant. Bubble polythene or straw around the container helps stop the roots freezing, says the RHS.
  • Cover tender plants overnight. Use horticultural fleece, in a double layer on the coldest nights, or an old sheet. Heavier grades of fleece give about 2°C of protection, according to the RHS, so the most tender plants are safer under cover.
  • Bring the most tender plants in. Move them under temporary cover for the cold spell: a sheltered spot, a cold greenhouse or a cool conservatory.
  • Raise pots off wet floors. The RHS suggests standing containers on bricks or pot feet so the drainage holes stay clear.

Even hardy plants appreciate this in a pot. The RHS describes camellias as generally hardy, yet for container-grown ones it still suggests moving the pot nearer the house or into a sheltered corner, wrapping the plant in fleece, or moving it into a cold greenhouse in freezing spells. Camellia japonica flowers in late winter and early spring, and the RHS notes that spring frosts can damage blossom.

Should you water potted plants before a frost?

Generally, no. The RHS advises avoiding watering containers when frost is forecast, and warns that waterlogged compost, especially in winter, can cause roots to rot. Remove saucers and trays before winter so pots don’t stand in water, and if pots stay out all winter, choose frost-proof ones, as terracotta can crack.

That doesn’t mean letting everything dry to dust. The RHS notes that evergreens in containers may need watering during dry spells in winter. If the compost is dry, water on a milder day rather than just before a freezing night.

How to protect potted plants in a heat wave

The RHS suggests checking pots daily in warm or windy weather and twice a day when it is hot; in summer dry spells you may need to water every day or every other day. Feel the compost first, as the guide to how often to water explains.

  • Water early. The RHS recommends early morning or early evening, when less water evaporates from the compost. UC Agriculture and Natural Resources (UC ANR) suggests watering deeply in the early morning the day before the heat arrives.
  • Shade the midday sun. UC ANR advises blocking as much midday sun as possible: move pots into shade or against a shadier wall (north-facing in the northern hemisphere), or use 30 to 50% shade cloth.
  • Mulch the compost. Mulching pots reduces water lost by evaporation, says the RHS, though careful watering is still needed; UC ANR adds that mulch helps protect roots.
  • Mind dark and metal pots. Dark containers heat up fast in the sun, thin black plastic nursery pots included, and the RHS notes that metal pots heat up in sunshine, baking the compost. Keep them out of full sun.
  • Group pots and empty saucers. Grouping pots gives mutual shade and raises local humidity, says the RHS. On hot days, UC ANR suggests emptying saucers after watering, as shallow water heats up quickly, and checking the temperature of hose water before it reaches the roots.

Late frosts and early chills: the in-between nights

Frost isn’t only a winter problem. The RHS notes that late spring frosts still occur and can scorch or kill tender growth, and advises putting tender plants out only when nights stay consistently above 7°C, after two to three weeks of getting them used to outdoor conditions. In autumn the turn comes the other way: Iowa State Extension suggests bringing houseplants that summered outdoors back inside when nights dip consistently to around 10°C (50°F). For the wider rhythm of the year, see the seasonal houseplant care calendar.

Questions

Should I water my potted plants before a frost?

Generally no. The RHS advises avoiding watering containers when frost is forecast, because waterlogged compost in winter can rot roots. If evergreens in pots are dry during a winter dry spell, water them on a milder day instead.

Does horticultural fleece protect plants from frost?

It helps a little. The RHS says heavier grades of fleece give about 2°C of protection and suggests a double layer on the coldest nights. For pots, also wrap the container and group pots against a sheltered wall; the most tender plants are safer under cover.

How do I keep potted plants cool in a heat wave?

Water early in the morning (UC ANR suggests deeply, the day before the heat arrives), shade pots from the midday sun, mulch the compost, group pots together and keep dark or metal pots out of full sun. Check pots daily, and twice a day in hot weather.

When should I bring balcony houseplants inside in autumn?

Before the cold arrives. Iowa State University Extension suggests moving houseplants indoors when nights dip consistently to around 10°C (50°F), since many are damaged once nights fall to about 7°C. Check them for pests first.
