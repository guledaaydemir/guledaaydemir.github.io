How to Declutter Your iPhone Without Overcomplicating It

By Gül Eda Aydemir · Updated September 2026

Most iPhone decluttering advice fails for the same reason: it asks for a full-system overhaul. Colour-coded home screens, a folder taxonomy, a fresh start. That takes a weekend, and three weeks later everything is back.

A better approach is to fix the handful of things that actually affect how the phone feels day to day, and to accept that the rest is noise. This guide works through those in order of impact — notifications, unused apps, the camera roll, files, tabs, the home screen, and notes and messages — with a short version at the end for people who have fifteen minutes.

1. Notifications 10 min · highest impact

Start here, because it's the only item on this list that changes your experience of the phone every single hour.

Open Settings › Notifications and go down the list app by app. The test is simple: do I ever act on this? If you swipe it away without reading, turn it off entirely. Most people can switch off 60–80% of their notifications and lose nothing — delivery updates, game nudges, social apps announcing activity you'd have seen anyway, news alerts that aren't news.

Keep notifications for things where the timing matters: messages from actual people, calendar events, banking alerts, anything time-critical for work. For the borderline cases, use Scheduled Summary — the notifications still arrive, just batched at times you choose instead of interrupting you.

Also worth five seconds: turn off badges for apps where the red number is an anxiety generator rather than information. Email is the usual offender.

2. Unused apps 10 min

Open Settings › General › iPhone Storage. Besides showing sizes, this screen lists when you last used each app, which removes the guesswork.

Anything you haven't opened in six months is a deletion candidate. If you're hesitant because of the data inside it, use Offload App instead — the app is removed but its documents and settings stay, and reinstalling restores them.

Two categories worth special attention. Duplicated functionality: three note apps, two to-do apps, four photo editors. Pick one of each and delete the rest; the decision fatigue of choosing between them is its own form of clutter. And apps installed for a single occasion — an event app, a rental scooter service from a holiday, a one-time ticketing app. These are pure residue.

3. The camera roll Ongoing · short sessions

For most people this is the largest single source of digital clutter, and it's the one that behaves least like the others. Notifications and apps are a one-time fix. The camera roll refills every week, so it needs a routine rather than a purge.

Don't attempt the whole library. It has no visible end, which is precisely why previous attempts failed. Instead:

  • Work in batches with a defined end. One day, one month, one event, or one category — not "All Photos".
  • Start with the categories that need no thought. Screenshots first: open Photos › Albums › Screenshots and clear the ones whose purpose expired. Then bursts, then near-identical retakes.
  • Check videos separately. They hold most of the storage even though they're a small share of the item count — the storage guide covers the ordering.
  • Use the built-in Duplicates album. Under Photos › Albums › Utilities, if iOS found exact duplicates, each group has a Merge option that keeps the best version.
  • Decide fast. Two seconds per photo. Anything that takes longer gets skipped, not deliberated.
  • Empty Recently Deleted when you're confident. Deleted items sit there for 30 days and keep occupying storage until they're removed.

4. Downloads and files 5 min

The Files app is where PDFs go to die. Open it and check two places: On My iPhone › Downloads and your iCloud Drive root.

Typical contents: boarding passes from trips already taken, contracts you signed, manuals for appliances you no longer own, a dozen images saved from a browser. Sort by date, work backwards, delete the expired.

If you want a lightweight structure rather than none, three folders handle almost everything: Current (active), Reference (keep permanently — warranties, contracts, IDs), Archive (finished but not deletable). More granularity than that becomes its own maintenance burden.

5. Browser tabs 2 min

If you have 400 open Safari tabs, none of them are being read. They're a to-do list you never agreed to.

The honest approach: skim for anything genuinely useful, save those two or three to bookmarks or a note, then close all tabs at once — long-press the tabs button in Safari and choose to close them all.

To stop it recurring, open Settings › Apps › Safari › Close Tabs and set tabs to close automatically after a day, week or month. This single setting prevents the problem permanently, and nothing valuable is ever lost — anything that mattered would have been bookmarked.

6. The home screen 10 min

This is the part people over-engineer. You don't need a colour system or custom icons.

A simple structure that holds up: keep one home screen containing the apps you genuinely use daily, plus a dock. Everything else lives in the App Library, which is searchable — you reach rarely-used apps by swiping down and typing, which is faster than hunting through folders anyway. Extra pages can be hidden by long-pressing the screen and tapping the page dots.

Widgets deserve a quick audit. A widget you no longer read is taking prime screen space for nothing. Keep the one or two you actually glance at and remove the rest.

7. Notes and messages 10 min

Notes accumulate scratch material: half-written lists, a Wi-Fi password from a hotel, an idea you already acted on. Sort by date modified, work backwards through anything untouched for six months, and delete what's been resolved. Notes are cheap in storage but expensive in search — a cluttered Notes app makes the useful notes harder to find.

Messages are worth checking for a different reason. Long threads with photos and videos attached can use a surprising amount of storage. Check Settings › General › iPhone Storage › Messages to see the breakdown by attachment type, and consider setting messages to auto-delete after a year if you don't need permanent history.

The fifteen-minute version

If you only do one pass and never come back, do these:

  • Turn off every notification you don't act on (10 min — by far the biggest change)
  • Close all browser tabs and set them to auto-close after a week (2 min)
  • Clear the Screenshots album in Photos (3 min)

That's it. Apps, files and the home screen can wait; none of them affect your day the way notifications do.

Keeping it that way

Decluttering once is easy. The reason phones drift back is that each area refills at a different rate, and treating them all as one annual project guarantees that none of them get handled.

A rough division that works:

  • Set once, forget: notification settings, auto-closing tabs, message auto-deletion. Configure them and they keep working.
  • Weekly, briefly: the camera roll and downloads. These refill fastest, and a short recurring pass keeps both from ever building a backlog — this is what a Sunday digital reset is for.
  • Quarterly: apps, home screen, notes. Slow accumulation, so a check every few months is enough.

And a note on scope: a tidy phone isn't the goal in itself. The goal is not losing five minutes to finding a photo, not having storage warnings interrupt a video, and not being pulled out of what you're doing by a notification that didn't matter. Optimise for those, and ignore the rest.
